# Design Signals — Reference

Source: Glare | Design Signal v1.3 (master), May 3, 2026.

## Opening frame

Design signals turn user input into decisions.

In product and design work, teams have no shortage of data. Dashboards show what happened. Analytics track behavior. Feedback captures what users say. Teams can see what users did and how they reacted, but decisions still slow down.

The gap is not information. It is direction. **Design signals close that gap by connecting what users do to what the team should do next.**

## What is a Design Signal

A design signal is a **measurable input from users that makes the invisible obvious**.

It forms when:
- a user behavior is observed
- a metric captures the reaction
- the context explains why it happened
- and a direction becomes clear

On their own, the pieces are incomplete:
- A metric shows what happened
- Feedback describes what users said
- Intuition suggests what might be wrong

A signal connects them into something the team can use.

- **Output:** a clear understanding of what is happening
- **Decision:** what to do next becomes easier to see

## Why signals matter

Teams generate plenty of ideas, but progress slows when there isn't a clear path for deciding what to do next. Metrics get reviewed but not applied. Feedback is collected but not connected. Conversations continue without reaching a clear outcome, and momentum starts to fade.

Signals bring clarity into the work. They help teams:
- understand what is actually happening
- reduce reliance on opinion
- make tradeoffs visible
- move forward with direction

- **Output:** shared clarity across the team
- **Decision:** faster, more confident choices

## Signals as a working layer

Signals are not just inputs. They are the layer that **connects user experience to product decisions**. In Glare, signals connect:
- user behavior
- UX metrics
- design decisions
- business outcomes

They turn observation into action by making it clear how the work should move forward.

- **Input:** user reactions, behavior, feedback
- **Process:** structure, connect, and evaluate
- **Output:** direction that can be acted on

## How to use Design Signals — the four sub-sections

The Design Signals section is broken into four parts. Each one solves a different part of the problem.

### 1. Components — How a signal is built and strengthened

Signals are only useful when they are complete and connected. A partial signal creates more interpretation, not less.

Covers:
- the structure of a complete signal (six parts)
- techniques to clarify and connect it

**Use this to:** turn raw input into a usable signal.

### 2. Signal Types — Where signals apply across the experience

Signals need to cover more than a single moment. A strong decision depends on understanding how an idea holds up across the full experience.

Organizes signals into four types:
- **Need** (credibility) — does this connect to a real user need
- **Use** (usefulness) — does this make sense to users
- **Prefer** (desirability) — do users want this direction
- **Adopt** (behavior) — does this work in real behavior

Together, they provide a more complete view of what is happening and where to focus.

**Use this to:** ensure signals cover the full experience.

### 3. Signal Quality — Whether a signal can be trusted

Not everything that looks like a signal can guide a decision. Some inputs show movement but don't explain what it means or what to do next.

Covers:
- is this a signal or just a metric
- does it connect behavior to meaning
- does it point to a decision

Signals are treated as a simple filter: **usable** or **not usable**. This keeps decisions grounded in evidence rather than interpretation.

**Use this to:** decide if a signal is strong enough to act on.

### 4. Capturing Signals — How signals are created and applied

Signals are most valuable when they are part of the work, not something added after the fact. They should form as ideas are explored, not after decisions are already made.

Covers:
- gather input from users
- structure it with UX metrics
- connect it to decisions
- build patterns over time

Over time, this creates continuity. Each signal builds on the last instead of restarting from scratch.

**Use this to:** create signals as part of everyday work.

## How this fits together

Each part builds toward the same outcome: helping teams decide what to do next.

Signals start as input, take shape through structure, are evaluated for quality, and are applied to real decisions. As this repeats, the work becomes clearer and more consistent.

Signals are most useful when treated as **questions first**. They help surface what is happening, where the work is breaking down, and what needs to change.

That is what turns data into direction and keeps decisions moving.
