# Get Started — Compressed

## What this block is about

The Get Started block is the on-ramp into Glare, ZURB's decision framework for product and design teams. Its job is to introduce *what Glare is* (a framework that turns user input into "design signals" connecting user needs, UX metrics, and business goals), *why it exists* (decisions stall not at ideation but at the moment of choice — research lands too late, opinions overpower evidence, and AI has accelerated output past judgment), and *how to start using it today* without adopting heavy methodology. The block delivers an introduction, a "Why it Matters" stakes essay, a six-step starter sequence, instruction on reading signals, an explanation of the framework's layered structure, guidance on proving design impact, scenario-based application examples, an assessment, a community invitation, a personal note from founder Bryan, plus internal planning artifacts (outline, big ideas, blockers, Ray Tools spec). Across all docs, the recurring promise is the same: clarity in the moment a decision needs to be made, by reducing scattered inputs into a single shareable signal that aligns user need + business goal + UX metric.

## Glare | Get Started, Introduction v1.2 (also v1.1)

- **Glare is a decision framework for product and design** that turns user input into design signals connecting what users need with what the business expects, delivering clarity at the moment of decision rather than weeks later in a report.
- It is **not another dashboard, research method, or delivery process**. It reshapes things teams already rely on (intuition, early research, rough concepts, open questions) into usable signals.
- After ~2,500 ZURB projects, the core finding is that no one needs more data or longer reports — they need clarity in the moment, knowing what to do next, and confidence before shipping.
- **Why Glare exists**: product/design rarely fails in ideation; it fails when decisions need to be made. Teams struggle to (a) describe user needs concretely, (b) frame the decision clearly, (c) capture a meaningful signal, (d) connect choice to what the business cares about. Tim Banks quote: feedback loops are too long, so teams compromise with progress metrics + small studies.
- **What makes Glare a framework** (v1.2): brings together three things normally treated separately:
  - **Design signals** — leverage; specific, measurable cues tied to a decision answering "why this, why now?"
  - **Organization** — five constraints/amplifiers (Organizing Work, Managing Complexity, Building Proof, Guiding Decisions, Scaling Influence) that determine whether leverage becomes impact.
  - **Movement** — four core design activities: Define, Measure, Focus, Lead.
- **You don't "do Glare." You use it.** Adaptable entry points: a hunch, a metric, a concept review, or a business request. ~16 building blocks; pick what helps.
- **Problems Glare solves**: messy/disconnected data; opinion-driven decisions; can't show design impact; misalignment on user needs; speed-vs-clarity pressure (AI generates output faster than judgment can validate it).
- **Who Glare is for**: design leaders proving value, PMs needing clarity faster than roadmaps allow, research leaders wanting insights to shape decisions, founders/execs wanting fast credible evidence, designers wanting their thinking to influence outcomes.
- **Origin**: built by ZURB from millions of Helio responses + 50,000+ hours of testing.

## Glare | Get Started, Why it Matters V1.1

- **Thesis**: design only works when teams can make clear decisions; the real cost of unclear decisions is **confidence**, and once trust breaks, momentum breaks.
- **Reality inside teams**: ideas die not because they're weak but because **decisions stall**. Stalling starts unannounced (stakeholders dip in/out, goals half-formed, fuzzy user needs). Teams shift from understanding to output; shipping replaces learning. Bill Saconne quote on cutting through opinions and ego.
- **Why design struggles**: no shared signals; data scattered; metrics arrive too late; outcomes feel disconnected; without a shared problem definition, expertise turns into noise.
- **The 10 blockers that stop designers from collecting signals** (verbatim):
  1. No access to real users
  2. User needs are unclear
  3. Audiences are undefined
  4. Questions are weak or biased
  5. Concepts are not testable
  6. Research is too slow
  7. Signals arrive too late
  8. Data does not guide decisions
  9. Teams do not trust the data
  10. No shared UX metrics
- **Cost of unclear decisions**: rework hurts, but wasted time does more damage. Releases slow, confidence drains, silos form, culture breaks before the product does. Dave Huber quote: "Teams confuse motion with progress."
- **The 10 blockers that prevent the business from capitalizing on design** (verbatim):
  1. Work moves without clear user value
  2. Roadmaps shaped by opinions, not evidence
  3. Metrics arrive after decisions
  4. Research does not feed the work in time
  5. Teams operate with different truths
  6. Good ideas stall in meetings
  7. Leaders cannot see design's impact
  8. Slow decisions kill momentum
  9. Investment goes to the safest option
  10. The team cannot show what moved outcomes
- **Why now**: AI accelerates output but outpaces judgment; without shared signals, teams burn out or freeze. Glare brings attention back to the user at the moment of decision; it's non-linear — start where the gap is.

## Glare | Get Started, What to do first v1.1

The six-step starter sequence to give teams an immediate foothold without learning the whole framework:

**Step 1: Pick a user need** — Surface the kind of struggle users are having so the team can stop debating everything. Use the four anchor questions:
1. Do users like this?
2. Does it make sense?
3. Can they rely on it?
4. Can they complete what they came to do?

This is a focusing move, not a research step.

**Step 2: Choose one metric** — Gives shape to a fuzzy need; turns "this isn't working" into "how well is this working right now?" Pick the metric that matches the debate:
- Clarity issue → comprehension
- Not drawn to it → sentiment
- Flow feels wrong → completion
- Trust in question → reliability or satisfaction

**Step 3: Run a test** — Dashboards tell what happened after the fact and can't test intent. A focused test gives narrow feedback against the chosen metric. A good fast test delivers: feedback tied to the user struggle, responses from the right audience, a shared reference point, answers in minutes with patterns visible in a day.

**Step 4: Turn the metric into a signal** — A number alone isn't a signal; a signal is a number that tells you what to do next. Connect three things: the user struggle, the design intent, the business goal. Examples:
- Low comprehension → copy/hierarchy/structure asks too much.
- Poor first-click performance → page hierarchy doesn't match the desired action.
- Weak sentiment → value isn't landing with target audience.

**Step 5: Share the signal** — Strong signals travel lightly. Present as a small focused story:
- Start with the problem in plain language.
- Show the metric that points to it.
- Add 1–2 supporting cues only if they strengthen the pattern.
- Recommend the change and explain why it matters now.

Don't over-explain or defend methodology. Make the next step obvious.

**Step 6: Use the signal in a meeting** — Bring it in with intention: present user need, show metric, explain in plain language, recommend next step, invite questions while keeping the conversation oriented toward a decision. Pushback means the signal is working. Your role is to guide the decision, not defend the data.

**Why this works**: gives teams a shared point of reference; designers regain influence, product feels safer, engineers/marketers align, leaders gain confidence. When confidence breaks, momentum slows; signals rebuild confidence by making decisions clearer and easier to trust.

## Glare | Get Started, How to Read signals (Nov 30, 2025)

- **Core distinction**: UX metrics tell you what happened; signals tell you what to do next. A metric is a datapoint; a signal is direction. Signals turn "I think" into "we know."
- **What makes a metric become a signal**: connection to a user need, design intention, business outcome, and the user's context.
- **Why signals matter**: give clarity early, sharpen intuition with evidence (instead of fighting it), reveal where the journey is breaking, tie UX work to business outcomes. "One signal can stop wasted effort. Ten signals can build confidence. A hundred signals can shape strategy."
- **The six pieces of a strong signal** (Glare signal model):
  1. **Design intuition** — your informed pattern recognition.
  2. **UX metric** — the raw measurement (appeal, comprehension, clarity, satisfaction, usability).
  3. **User needs** — what the user was actually trying to accomplish.
  4. **Context** — the moment, layout, copy, hierarchy, step in flow; the *why* behind the number.
  5. **Business goals** — what the org needs this screen/flow to move.
  6. **Direction** — synthesis of the other pieces; clarity to move forward.

  A signal emerges only when all six form a coherent story.

- **Good signals**: clear, actionable; show why the number is what it is, what part of design influenced it, alignment with user goal, support of business goal, and what to do next.
- **Weak signals**: metrics interpreted alone; screens evaluated without comparison; sentiment improves but behavior doesn't; behavior improves but performance flat; local clarity never becomes journey clarity. They create movement without direction.
- **Comparison is the first move** — without comparison you don't know if a score is good or merely the best available; comparison reveals tradeoffs.
- **Signal layers map** (cause and effect):
  - Leading UX metrics → behavioral intent → performance → product outcomes → business results.
  - Local signal breaks at screen level; systemic signal breaks across journey; operational break = product improves but business metric doesn't move.
- **Signals plug into existing rituals**: backlog grooming, roadmap reviews, sprint reviews, release go/no-go. Example: testing two dashboard versions showed one was 30% clearer on task success — ended debate, guided release.
- **Holding principle**: treat every signal as a question before treating it as proof. Signals create movement; questions turn movement into direction.

## Glare | Get Started, How Glare Works (Dec 1, 2025) — The Five Layers

This earlier/parallel version explains Glare as a five-layered system. The layers exist because team problems sit at different altitudes — human, organizational, structural, informational, decision-flow.

- **Layer 1: Human Reality** — *the reason the work exists*. Anchors purpose. Provides: clear picture of what people are trying to accomplish; tension between user needs and business goals; the four universal questions; an anchor for designers when chaos hits. Why first: every other layer serves this; without it, the system becomes disconnected technique.

- **Layer 2: Dimensions (Organizational Capability)** — *the reason work succeeds or fails inside an org*. Provides: five viewports for diagnosing why teams get stuck; muscles to convert insight into action; a model for working in the org you have. The five capabilities:
  - Organizing Work
  - Managing Complexity
  - Building Proof
  - Guiding Decisions
  - Scaling Influence

- **Layer 3: Signals** — *the reason the framework is practical*. A small local unit of clarity; immediate evidence tied to the design problem; consistent method for breaking deadlock. Without signals, Glare becomes theory.

- **Layer 4: User Data** — *the reason signals are credible*. Intentional behavioral, attitudinal, performance inputs shaped to the decision (not inherited from analytics). Sits under signals because signals are only as strong as their data.

- **Layer 5: Glare OS** — *the reason the system stays coherent*. Defines how signals flow, blocks connect, patterns repeat, sequences shape decisions. The engine that makes Glare a unified system; foundation AI agents can use; keeps the framework running so teams don't have to hold the structure in their heads.

- **How they work together (closed loop)**: Human Reality defines what matters → Dimensions show capability → Signals create the next move → User Data supplies evidence → OS keeps everything coherent and repeatable. The point isn't to follow them in order; it's to understand how they feed each other.

## Glare | Get Started, How to prove Design Impact (Dec 1, 2025)

- **Thesis**: design loses power when it can't show what it changes. Mapping fixes this — shows how a design choice travels through experience, into product performance, into business results.
- **The Design Impact Chain** (verbatim backbone diagram):

  **User Needs → User Outcomes (Design KPIs) → Product Outcomes (Product KPIs) → Business Outcomes (Business KPIs) → Business Goals**

- **Sequence in practice**:
  - **Design choices** = your design intent, shaped by the value you believe it'll create.
  - **UX metrics → User Outcomes** = clear measurements (Does it get noticed? Make sense? Do people want it? Can they complete it?). These become design-level KPIs.
  - **Signals** = leading indicators showing early lift before analytics catch up.
  - **Product KPIs → Product Outcomes** = adoption, activation, time on task, engagement.
  - **Business outcomes** = lagging indicators (retention, revenue, loyalty, cost to serve).
  - **Business goals** = strategic targets the org cares about.
- **What leaders want to see**: not your layout, but **risk signals** (where experience hurts adoption/trust/friction) and **opportunity signals** (where clarity/speed/confidence create lift). Leaders reward proof, not polish.
- **How Glare creates proof**:
  - Makes value visible sooner (one signal can reveal lift/friction before engineering commits weeks).
  - Connects layers of work (design intent → outcomes → impact as one chain).
  - Keeps attribution honest (consistent metrics; prevents noise-as-impact).
  - Builds confidence through the **four facets**: credibility, usefulness, desirability, viability — they act as gates.
  - Turns signals into influence by being clear and timely.
- **Examples tied to real metrics**:
  - **Navigation clarity → retention** (find what they need faster, stay longer, return more).
  - **Checkout usability → revenue** (less hesitation, fewer errors, more transactions).
  - **Onboarding comprehension → adoption** (understanding from start improves activation, lowers churn).
  - System-wide: fewer support tickets, smoother cross-team workflow, lower cost to serve, clearer PMF, stronger brand loyalty, better investment decisions.

## Glare | Get Started, Start Using it Today (Dec 1, 2025)

Scenarios for folding signals into existing rituals without overhaul:

- **Use signals in your backlog** — three immediate effects: weak ideas die faster (no user evidence); user stories sharpen (signal becomes the reason); priorities re-rank (impact visible). Backlog becomes a list of outcomes, not opinions.
- **Use signals in a roadmap review** — reduces meeting round-trips; clarifies size/shape of problems; shifts narrative from "someone wants this" to "users need this." Items without proof become bets, not assumptions.
- **Use signals in sprint refinement** — fifteen-minute sanity checks: test labels and copy, clarify component order, check comprehension on a single screen, sort hunches before sprint, apply one UX metric as quality check. Lightweight risk filter.
- **Use signals in release readiness** — the five core release signals:
  - **Comprehension**: do people understand it the first time?
  - **Trust**: does it feel credible enough to proceed?
  - **Completion**: can users finish without friction?
  - **Usability**: do hidden blockers appear when stitched together?
  - **Expectation match**: does messaging prepare users for what happens next?
- **Examples by role**:
  - **PM**: leverage before live traffic; faster, safer A/B test.
  - **Designer**: turns advocacy into evidence; work speaks for itself.
  - **Director**: see where to invest; expose process breakdowns.
  - **Founder**: change workflows fast; show user struggles analytics can't see.
- **Bridge to assessment**: signals are easy to start with but bottlenecks make them hard to sustain; the assessment shows where they'll create the fastest lift.

## Glare | Big Ideas (Nov 27, 2025)

Internal planning doc capturing the 16 foundational ideas of Glare.

1. **Core problem Glare solves**: designers can't tie design decisions to real business impact — not from lack of skill but inability to see user needs, see business needs, measure the space, turn intuition into something leaders trust, structure choices for org alignment.
2. **The bridging mechanism**: the design signal — smallest unit of clarity tying user need + business goal + UX metric + intuition.
3. **Atomic model insight**: design signal = atom; user needs/business goals/UX metrics = internal particles; hunches/concepts/questions/methods = orbiting electrons; assumptions = anti-particles destabilizing clarity; **Glare = the field that stabilizes the atom**.
4. **Real entry point**: teams enter Glare *the moment questions appear* — not when metrics are chosen or concepts finished. The collision point of designer intuition + researcher needs + PM goals + leader outcomes + everyone's assumptions.
5. **First move**: shape the question into a decision frame — reveals what's being decided, the hunch, the user need it touches, the business goal, the uncertainty, the stakes.
6. **Second move**: shape the concept to express the decision — prevents drift, premature screens, untestable ideas, mismatched expectations, false signals.
7. **Birth of the signal**: emerges only when the shaped concept is placed in a context where users interact, metrics measure, and a gap becomes visible.
8. **Meaning of the signal**: expose the gap between what the team expected and what users showed. Cited research: Elizabeth Newton's "Tappers and Listeners" experiment — tappers predicted 50% success rate, actual was 2.5% (the curse of knowledge gap).
9. **Refinement engine**: signal → gap → refinement → stronger signal → clearer decision. The self-reinforcing loop is the heart of the system.
10. **Comparison move**: a single signal is interesting; comparison makes it meaningful. Defines which version is better, what better means, performance shifts, whether to advance, competitor performance, audience differences, mobile vs desktop.
11. **Implications layer**: comparison doesn't directly produce a decision — surface implications for the next initiative (engineering constraints, workflow timing, strategy, agendas, release cycles, cross-functional realities).
12. **Decision point**: advance, refine again, kill, scope differently, pivot, reprioritize.
13. **Path to business impact**: anchor decisions to business outcomes, product KPIs, strategic goals, retention/revenue/efficiency/trust.
14. **Reinforcement of intuition**: capturing patterns, validating instincts, refining judgment, creating a library of learned signals.
15. **True value statement**: "A clear way to make decisions that connect user needs to business goals through stronger signals."
16. **Emotional transformation for new user**: "I finally understand why our decisions stall and what to do next."

## Glare | Blockers to Glare (Nov 27, 2025)

Internal synthesis of nine systemic blockers Glare aims to resolve. Each block listed with core issues:

1. **Messy platforms and weak data** — the environment is too messy to measure: fragmented analytics, missing instrumentation, legacy platforms, weak/inconsistent data, low volume, hard to scale research.
2. **No shared metrics or baselines** — design and business don't share the definition of success: no shared metrics, misaligned KPIs, no baseline/before-after proof, can't tie design to outcomes, design language doesn't translate.
3. **Research does not translate into decisions** — signals die in the handoff: scattered findings, no clear measurement owners, friction-filled handoffs, political decision-making, debates about artifacts not outcomes, slow processes.
4. **Speed pressures create compounding debt** — shipping before validating, inconsistent quality, burnout, incoherent flows, fear of rework/design debt.
5. **AI expectations outpace skills** — confusion about what to automate, fear of low-quality AI output, lack of AI-accelerated design skills, ethics/trust/safety concerns.
6. **Design cannot prove value in business terms** — hard to isolate design impact, difficult to measure intangibles like trust/emotion, limited causality, executives default to gut.
7. **Weak alignment / fragile cross-functional collab** — misaligned incentives, competing priorities, fragmented workflows, leaders explaining design instead of advancing it.
8. **No consistent signal collection or reuse** — inconsistent measurement, no repeatable testing cadence, no method for turning signals into action, early-stage teams have too few signals.
9. **Designers lack visibility, influence, impact path** — defending craft instead of impact, poor org visibility, gig work instability, AI-shifting career identity.

Note: doc explicitly says the assessment indirectly highlights these (except some marked red), and "we need a stronger through line to the framework."

## Glare | Ray Tools (Jan 25, 2026)

Engineering spec for "Ray," apparently an AI agent / system component implementing the Glare loop. Defines seven primitives in pipeline order:

**Full loop**: Audience → Metrics → Tests → Observations → Findings → Signals → Decisions

For each, the spec defines: Inputs, State, Triggers, Rules, Guardrails, Thresholds, Outputs, "What Ray does," and an "Engineering shorthand."

1. **Audiences** — make sure every signal comes from the right people. Inputs: audience definitions, segment attributes, sample size. Outputs: validity check, "audience mismatch" warnings. Engineering shorthand: *filter + validator applied before interpretation*.
2. **UX Metrics** — ensure teams measure the right thing for the risk. Rules: which metrics apply to which question types. Guardrails: warn when metrics don't match the decision. Outputs: metric adequacy assessment, missing-metric suggestions. Shorthand: *semantic lenses over raw data*.
3. **Tests** — turn ideas into focused, answerable bets. Rules: one primary question per test, metric-test alignment. Outputs: test clarity score, "overloaded test" warnings. Shorthand: *containers for intent, not just data collection*.
4. **Observations** — describe what happened without interpretation creep. Rules: no recommendations allowed here. Outputs: structured observation statements, deltas/anomalies. Shorthand: *lossless summaries of results*.
5. **Findings** — explain why observations matter. Rules: must reference a specific observation. State: linked decision intent. Outputs: finding statements, confidence labels (strong/moderate/weak). Shorthand: *interpreted observations with constraints*.
6. **Signals** — identify evidence strong enough to guide action. Thresholds: signal strength, replication count. State: signal history per initiative. Outputs: signal objects, confidence rating. Shorthand: *promoted findings with memory*.
7. **Decisions** — turn signals into commitment. Rules: explicit tradeoff articulation. Guardrails: no decision without a signal reference. Outputs: decision recommendation, locked vs flexible requirements. Shorthand: *signals + constraints + intent*.

- **Leverage** — the ability for a design decision to move beyond the team that made it; created by signals, amplified or constrained by organizational dimensions.
- **Local vs systemic vs operational signal** — local breaks at screen level; systemic breaks across journey; operational = product improves but business metric doesn't move.
- **Leading vs lagging metrics** — leading UX metrics signal lift early; lagging business outcomes (retention, revenue, loyalty, cost to serve) move slowly.
- **Risk signals / Opportunity signals** — what executives scan for: friction-creating areas vs. lift-creating areas.
- **Ray** — engineering name for the system/agent implementing Glare's pipeline (Audience → Metrics → Tests → Observations → Findings → Signals → Decisions).
- **Helio** — ZURB's user-testing tool, a complementary product to Glare; source of millions of participant responses underlying the framework.
- **Foundation** — ZURB's earlier open-source responsive design system; cited as precedent for Glare's open community model.
- **Tappers and Listeners** — Elizabeth Newton's experiment cited for the curse-of-knowledge gap; tappers predicted 50% success, actual was 2.5%.
- **The 16 blocks** — the ~16 core ideas inside Glare; each can be used independently and tactically.
- **Glare OS** — the underlying logic engine defining how signals flow, how blocks connect, how patterns repeat; the layer AI agents can operate on.
- **Diamond of Clarity** — referenced as a system within the framework (alongside Signal Triangle, decision loops, comparison patterns) but not deeply defined in this block.

## Onboarding flow

The official four-page Get Started walkthrough teams should be guided through in order. Each builds the floor for the next.

**1. Introduction (v1.2 — "What Glare Is")** — Opens with the one-line pitch: Glare is a decision framework for product/design that turns user input into design signals connecting user need + business expectation, delivering clarity *at the moment of decision* rather than weeks later. It is explicitly not a dashboard, research method, or delivery process. The 2,500-project finding: nobody is asking for more data or longer reports — they want clarity in the moment, and confidence before shipping. AI made output cheap; confidence in that output stayed expensive.

The page then defines what makes Glare a framework — three things normally separated: **design signals** (leverage; specific measurable cues answering "why this, why now?"), **organization** (five constraints/amplifiers: Organizing Work, Managing Complexity, Building Proof, Guiding Decisions, Scaling Influence — they decide whether leverage becomes impact), and **movement** (four core activities: Define, Measure, Focus, Lead). Closes with the audience list (design leaders, PMs, research leaders, founders, designers) and "you don't *do* Glare, you *use* it" — entry points are a hunch, a metric, a concept review, or a business request.

**2. How Glare Works (v1.1 + Dec 1 five-layer doc)** — The working model. Three operating moves: read design signals → organize teams around signals → prove design impact. The "system effect" capstone: clarity (signals) + alignment (structure) + proof (outcomes) reinforce each other so momentum returns and learning compounds.

The deeper Dec 1 page expands this into the **five-layer closed loop**: Human Reality (anchors purpose, the four universal questions) → Dimensions (organizational capability, the five viewports for diagnosing why teams stall) → Signals (the smallest local unit of clarity, makes the framework practical) → User Data (intentional inputs shaped to the decision, not inherited from analytics) → Glare OS (the logic engine — flow rules, block connections, repeating patterns; the layer AI agents operate on). The point isn't sequence; it's understanding how the layers feed each other.

**3. Take the Assessment (Dec 1)** — A ~20-minute self-scoring tool at `my.helio.app/t/01K5SCHHQR5FP15E0GWZA0X42N` that surfaces where clarity breaks down, why decisions stall, and how signals move through the org. Maps to the Dimensions layer. Reveals strengths (where clarity is created), gaps (where proof fades), and crucially **shape over score** — a high-low imbalance reveals more than the average. Gives a small, repeatable next move and a link into the dimension that will create the fastest lift. Not fault-finding; finding the smallest leverage point.

**4. Start Using It Today (Dec 1, plus v1.1 marketing-site addition)** — Five drop-in scenarios for folding signals into existing rituals without overhaul: backlog (weak ideas die faster, stories sharpen, priorities re-rank), roadmap review (fewer round-trips, items without proof become bets not assumptions), sprint refinement (15-minute sanity checks on labels, copy, comprehension, hierarchy), release readiness (the five core release signals: comprehension, trust, completion, usability, expectation match), and marketing-website updates (validate messaging before scaling traffic; align brand/product/growth around shared evidence). Closes with the bridge back to the assessment: signals are easy to start, but bottlenecks make them hard to sustain — the assessment shows where lift will be fastest.

## Glare Assessment

The Helio Glare Assessment is the tool teams use to diagnose how clearly design signals move through their organization. It is hosted at `my.helio.app` and takes ~20 minutes. Use it whenever a user wants to know "where do we stand?" or "where should we start?" The Assessment is the bridge between the Get Started block and the Dimensions layer — it gives teams a shared map before they pick a next move.

### How it's scored

Every team gets scored on **five dimensions** (below). Each dimension is itself scored using the **four facets of clarity** (Define, Measure, Focus, Show), producing a **16-square grid** per dimension that maps where signals build vs. break down. Across Glare assessments most teams land between 68 and 85; above 90 = repeatable clear decision flow; below 70 = intuition still drives too much of the process.

Each dimension's report has a fixed five-section structure: (1) Overview & Score, (2) How It's Measured, (3) Rubric Breakdown (the 16-square grid filled in), (4) Strengths and Gaps, (5) Next Actions. The deliverable is not a number — it's a **shape**. A high score in one dimension paired with a low one in another is more diagnostic than the average. Teams should look at the gap between their highest and lowest quadrants first; that gap usually points to the biggest opportunity.

### The five dimensions evaluated

1. **Guiding Decisions** — How confidently the team turns user insights into choices leaders believe in. Strong = ideas move quickly from curiosity to proof, intuition is balanced with validation. Weak = debates repeat, validation slows, the loudest opinion wins. Fix: build faster feedback loops that turn early signals into measurable validation.

2. **Making Impact (Building Proof)** — How design connects outcomes to measurable business results. Strong = proof is visible, repeatable, trusted; teams can show how design moved the metric. Weak = wins stay local inside the design team and never travel. Fix: link intent to outcomes upfront, share proof broadly, make success criteria measurable.

3. **Scaling Influence** — How design proof travels from contributors → experts → leaders. Strong = signals move freely across levels; design earns strategic weight. Weak = results stay tactical and trapped in design; leadership sees effort but not impact. Fix: strengthen translation across roles; make signals reusable by experts and leaders.

4. **Organizing Work** — How teams document, store, and reuse design results. Strong = knowledge compounds through structure; lessons carry forward. Weak = teams repeat research, lose insights, rebuild proof from scratch. Fix: turn documentation into evidence libraries that support faster decisions.

5. **Managing Complexity** — How teams stay adaptable under pressure, growth, or change. Strong = structure and trust let teams navigate uncertainty; clear frameworks prevent chaos. Weak = ambiguity stalls decisions and blurs roles. Fix: build shared frameworks and rhythms that sustain clarity through change.

### The four-quadrant model (worked example: Guiding Decisions)

Inside each dimension, scores spread across four quadrants formed by two tensions. Horizontal axis: **Effectual ↔ Causal** (exploration vs. outcome focus). Vertical axis: **Abstract ↔ Certain** (concept formation vs. validation). The four quadrants:

- **Effectual + Abstract** — the curiosity / discovery phase. Strong = deep research drives early opportunity. Weak = insights stay abstract, never become validated direction.
- **Causal + Abstract** — the alignment phase. Strong = direction aligns across design/product/leadership. Weak = intent is clear but evidence is missing; ideas advance without proof.
- **Effectual + Certain** — the validation phase. Strong = active testing loops, learning is shared. Weak = validation happens but isn't tied to business outcomes.
- **Causal + Certain** — the proof phase. Strong = decisions are backed by trusted evidence; design shapes business direction. Weak = results exist but aren't communicated; proof never reaches leadership.

**Reading the spread**: scores within a 10-point range = healthy flow. A 15–20+ point spread = friction (usually validation-to-proof or contributor-to-leader). Top-heavy (Abstract/Effectual high) = curiosity outruns validation. Bottom-heavy (Certain/Causal high) = process dominates and exploration fades.

### How to act on findings

The Path Forward (from the master Assessment doc) is a five-step plan:

1. **Book a Glare Debrief** — focused conversation to identify highest-leverage points, build a 30-day roadmap. (calendly.com/zurb/helio-glare-assessment)
2. **Review the report as a team** — pick one dimension to strengthen first, decide what daily progress looks like, agree on one ritual that makes proof easier to see.
3. **Build a 90-day plan** — one or two improvements per dimension, each tied to a facet (Define / Measure / Focus / Show), with clear owners. Examples: validation questions in design reviews, a shared evidence library, a weekly leadership share-out of signals.
4. **Run first tests** — pick one opportunity, validate with users via Helio. A single signal can confirm direction or end debate before resources are committed.
5. **Re-measure quarterly** — each new score shows how maturity evolves. The aim is to make clarity part of the team's culture, not a one-off initiative.

Guiding rule from the dimension docs: **do not chase perfect balance — chase healthy flow**. A small, visible improvement that lands in 30 days beats a perfect plan that never ships. After Guiding Decisions strengthens, the natural next dimension is **Making Impact** (turning evidence into outcomes leaders can see).
