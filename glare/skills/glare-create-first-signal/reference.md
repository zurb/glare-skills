# Create Your First Signal — Compressed (v1.3)

## What this walkthrough is for

The "first signal in one decision" on-ramp. Most teams already have everything they need to make a clear decision — intuition, direction, user input, business context — but those pieces live in different places (conversations, notes, feedback, assumptions). Creating a signal connects them. The walkthrough is for the moment a real decision is on the table and the team is circling it.

## The trigger pattern

A typical situation:
- You have two directions for a flow
- Design prefers one, Product leans another way, Engineering is thinking about complexity
- There's some feedback and a bit of data, but nothing clearly points to a decision
- The work has to keep moving, but the decision isn't sharp
- Things start to **Frankenstein**: revisited, loses clarity across teams, confidence drops

## What you're actually doing

Creating a signal means taking a hunch and giving it enough structure to guide a real decision. A signal comes from bringing together what the team already knows:

| Piece | What it is |
|---|---|
| **UX metric** | What you're trying to understand or improve |
| **Intuition** | What the team is noticing |
| **Direction** | The option(s) you're considering |
| **Context** | Where the work sits within a concept |
| **Business goal** | What outcome you're trying to move |
| **User need** | What users are trying to satisfy |

Once these connect, the team has something concrete to work from. Conversation gets clearer. Tradeoffs are easier to see. The decision has direction behind it.

## The six-move arc

### Move 1: Start with a Hunch

Every signal starts with something the team is already reacting to. It usually doesn't show up as a formal idea — it shows up in conversation:

- "this direction feels cleaner"
- "users might get stuck here"
- "this could improve conversion"

That's the hunch. At this point, everyone is reacting but not necessarily in the same way. The conversation expands, more opinions come in, the decision stays open.

**Goal:** make the hunch clear enough that the team is aligned on what they're actually reacting to.

### Move 2: Make It Clear

Write the hunch down. Keep it to one sentence. Focus on **what you think is happening, not why it's right**.

Templates:
- "users may not understand what to do on this screen"
- "this version may make the next step clearer"
- "users may not understand what action to take here"
- "this version may improve completion of the next step"

This step is small but it changes the conversation. Instead of reacting to a general feeling, the team has something specific to respond to. Now connect the hunch to two things that ground it:

1. **What the user needs**
2. **What the business is trying to move**

Use a **User Needs Skill** to clarify:
- what the user is trying to do
- what they expect to happen
- where the experience might break

Use a **Business Goals Skill** to define:
- what outcome matters
- what success looks like

The work is no longer just team perspective. It's connected to real user behavior and real outcomes.

### Move 3: Shape the Signal

Tighten the signal so it can guide a decision. Focus on **one** thing you want to understand. Refine the hunch into something more specific, then anchor it to a UX metric.

The four most useful UX metrics at this stage:

| Metric | When to use it |
|---|---|
| **Comprehension** | Does the work make sense at a glance? Are users understanding what's being shown? |
| **Usability** | Can users move through the experience without friction? |
| **Confidence** | Do users feel sure enough about the next step to take it? |
| **Desirability** | Is the work landing emotionally? Are users drawn to it? |

This gives the team a shared lens. Now when you look at the work, you're not just reacting — you're looking for something specific.

## Using a Design Signal

### Move 4: Use the Signal

Place the signal next to the directions you're considering and look at them together. You're no longer reviewing designs on their own; you're looking at how each direction holds up against something specific.

Two questions:
- **What is the signal telling us to look for?**
- **How does each option respond to that?**

Patterns surface:
- Sometimes one direction is clearly stronger
- Sometimes both options reveal gaps
- Sometimes the signal itself needs to be refined

If the signal still feels thin, bring in a small amount of input. Lightweight options:
- show both directions to a few users
- ask a focused question tied to the metric
- review existing feedback or behavior

This adds weight without slowing things down. Differences become visible:
- what is improving
- what is getting worse
- what still needs attention

The signal brings tradeoffs into view so the team can move forward.

### Move 5: Decide What Moves Forward

The decision becomes easier. The team can move forward with a shared understanding. That might mean:

- choosing one direction
- refining what's there
- stopping something that isn't working

The signal gives the team a shared way to decide. Instead of circling, the team can commit and move.

### Move 6: Make Decisions Hold

The moment the work moves forward, the decision starts to meander. New people get involved. Details change. Constraints show up. Questions come back. What felt clear can shift as work moves across teams.

**The signal is what helps the decision hold.** Keep it visible — not as documentation, but as a shared reference for *why* the decision was made. When questions come up, bring it back:

- what was the team trying to understand?
- what did the signal show?
- what direction did that lead to?

This keeps the team aligned without reworking the same decision from the beginning.

---

## What this looks like over time

As the loop repeats:
- decisions land faster
- conversations stay focused
- the team builds on what's already been learned instead of starting over
- decisions carry across teams
- fewer conversations restart from scratch
- signals connect across decisions
- direction holds as work moves forward

You don't need to apply this everywhere at once. **Start with one decision. Run the loop. Then repeat where it matters.**

---

## Common stalls and how to unstick them

| Stall | What to do |
|---|---|
| Team can't agree on the hunch | Pick the loudest one in conversation; write it as a sentence; iterate from there |
| Hunch feels too vague | Force one UX metric (comprehension/usability/confidence/desirability) — that's what tightens it |
| Signal points in two directions | The signal needs more refining; or the directions are solving different problems — call that out |
| No real users available | Use the smallest possible input — even 3-5 responses surface a pattern |
| Decision keeps coming back | The signal isn't visible enough — pin it where the work lives, not in a deck |

## Sentence templates for writing the signal

- "We need to understand whether users *[user need]* with this design, because *[business goal]*. We'll use *[UX metric]* to read it."
- "Two directions on *[the work]*. Hunch: *[one-sentence hunch]*. We'll compare them on *[UX metric]* to decide."
- "*[Direction A vs. Direction B]* — testing *[UX metric]* against *[user need]* to inform *[decision being made]*."
