# Design Assessment — Reading Patterns

Compressed from "Glare | Design Assessment, Reading Patterns v1.3" (May 3, 2026).

> "The pattern matters more than any single score."

## When to use

- When the user has Design Assessment results across all five dimensions and wants to interpret the *shape*, not the individual numbers.
- When dimension scores are uneven — strong in one place, weak in another — and the user is trying to understand what the pattern reveals.
- When the user describes a recognizable shape: "balanced and strong," "strong proof weak influence," "strong start weak finish," "high activity low impact."
- When one weak dimension seems to be dragging the others, and the question is which break to fix first.
- When two teams have the same total score but very different problems.

**Don't use when** the user wants to understand *how* scores are built (`glare-assessment-scoring`), is choosing a 30-day next move (`glare-assessment-using-results`), or is deep in one specific dimension — drop into that dimension's skill.

## Why patterns matter

Most teams want to know their score first. That is natural. A score gives the assessment a clear starting point. But the score is only useful if it leads to a better conversation.

A team might score high in one dimension and low in another. That does not automatically mean the low score is the first thing to fix. The real question is how that low score affects the rest of the system.

Some examples:

- **Strong Organizing Work + weak Scaling Influence** → knowledge may be captured, but proof is not traveling far enough.
- **Strong Building Proof + weak Guiding Decisions** → evidence exists, but it may not be shaping choices.
- **Strong Guiding Decisions + weak Building Proof** → teams may move fast, but proof may not be strong enough to defend the direction.
- **Strong Managing Complexity + weak Organizing Work** → teams may handle large work well, but learning may still get lost afterward.

The score shows the parts. The pattern shows the flow.

## What patterns reveal

Patterns reveal where design impact is getting blocked. They help teams see whether the issue is happening early, in the middle, or late in the work.

A pattern can show:

- where work starts clearly
- where learning gets lost
- where complexity slows progress
- where proof breaks down
- where decisions lose momentum
- where influence stops moving
- where one strong dimension is carrying the others
- where one weak dimension is creating drag

This helps teams avoid surface fixes. Instead of "Our score is low, so we need more process," the team can ask, "Where is the system actually breaking?"

## Common pattern types

These are not labels for the team. They are ways to understand where improvement should start.

### 1. Balanced and strong

All five dimensions score well. Knowledge is organized, complexity is handled, proof is built, decisions are guided by signals, evidence travels.

**What this suggests:**
- keep the system consistent
- share the model with other teams
- look for places to scale what works
- use the assessment as a benchmark over time

**Risk:** complacency. Strong systems still need maintenance as teams, tools, products, and leadership priorities change.

### 2. Balanced but low

All five dimensions in a similar range, all underdeveloped. Work relies on individual effort, informal conversations, and inconsistent practices.

**What this suggests:**
- start with one clear operating habit
- define how work begins
- capture signals in a simple, repeatable way
- connect findings to decisions
- avoid trying to improve every dimension at once

**Risk:** spreading effort too thin. Pick one area where improvement will create visible movement.

### 3. Strong start, weak finish

Early work is clear, but impact fades later. Strong Organizing Work or Managing Complexity, weaker Building Proof / Guiding Decisions / Scaling Influence.

**What this suggests:**
- connect early goals to measurable outcomes
- define what proof should look like before work begins
- turn findings into explicit decisions
- package results so leaders can use them

**Risk:** good work keeps happening without creating enough visible impact.

### 4. Weak start, strong finish

The team can create outcomes, but the early reasoning is unclear. Weaker Organizing Work or Managing Complexity, stronger Building Proof / Guiding Decisions / Scaling Influence. Often happens when strong individuals push work forward without enough shared structure.

**What this suggests:**
- clarify the objective before work begins
- document hunches and assumptions
- make dependencies visible sooner
- capture why decisions were made
- connect outcomes back to the original intent

**Risk:** success becomes hard to repeat because the system depends too much on people remembering how it happened.

### 5. Strong proof, weak influence

The team has evidence, but it does not travel far enough. Stronger Building Proof, weaker Scaling Influence.

**What this suggests:**
- translate proof into leadership language
- create reusable proof stories
- connect results to business goals
- share signals earlier in planning conversations
- build a simple format for design impact

**Risk:** design stays respected locally but does not shape broader priorities.

### 6. Strong decisions, weak proof

The team moves, but the evidence behind the movement is not strong enough. Stronger Guiding Decisions, weaker Building Proof. Decisions may rely too much on intuition, stakeholder confidence, or thin evidence.

**What this suggests:**
- define what proof is needed before deciding
- connect decisions to UX metrics
- capture baselines or comparisons
- show what changed for users
- make the evidence behind decisions easier to see

**Risk:** fast decisions become hard to defend later.

### 7. Strong organization, weak complexity

The team captures knowledge well, but larger work still gets tangled. Stronger Organizing Work, weaker Managing Complexity.

**What this suggests:**
- name the type of complexity earlier
- separate simple, complicated, and complex work
- clarify dependencies before they block progress
- define ownership across teams
- decide what needs process and what needs learning

**Risk:** documentation improves, but the work still feels heavy.

### 8. High activity, low impact

The team is producing a lot, but the work is not clearly changing outcomes. Many projects, tests, meetings, artifacts, reviews — but the connection between activity and impact is weak. Several dimensions mid-range, but Building Proof or Scaling Influence stays low.

**What this suggests:**
- reduce the number of things being tracked
- focus on fewer, clearer outcomes
- connect design work to user and business goals
- turn results into decision-ready proof
- stop measuring activity as a proxy for impact

**Risk:** the team looks busy while momentum quietly fades.

### 9. Local strength, limited scale

One team or group is working well, but the maturity does not spread. Strong design practices in one product area, team, or leadership group; other teams do not share the same methods, language, or evidence. Often shows up as uneven scores or inconsistent survey responses.

**What this suggests:**
- identify where the strongest behavior already exists
- turn that behavior into a reusable practice
- create shared formats for evidence and decisions
- help experts connect patterns across teams
- bring leaders into the proof earlier

**Risk:** design maturity stays dependent on isolated champions.

## How to read your pattern

Start with the full score set. Look across all five dimensions:

- Organizing Work
- Managing Complexity
- Building Proof
- Guiding Decisions
- Scaling Influence

Then ask:

- Which score is strongest?
- Which score is weakest?
- Which gap is largest?
- Which dimension creates the most drag?
- Which strength can support the next improvement?
- Is the issue early, middle, or late in the system?
- What would create the most visible movement in the next 30 days?

Do not look only for the lowest number. Look for the **break that affects the whole system.**

## How patterns guide action

Patterns help teams choose where to focus. A weak dimension may need attention, but the best first move is usually the one that unlocks movement across several dimensions.

For example:

- If proof is strong but influence is weak → focus on **Scaling Influence**
- If decisions are slow because evidence is unclear → focus on **Building Proof**
- If work gets tangled before decisions happen → focus on **Managing Complexity**
- If learning keeps disappearing → focus on **Organizing Work**
- If evidence exists but choices keep circling → focus on **Guiding Decisions**

The goal is not to improve every score at once, but to make the next improvement obvious.

## What this does not mean

A pattern is not a permanent label. It is a current read on how design impact is moving through the organization. Patterns can change as teams improve how they capture evidence, guide decisions, build proof, manage complexity, and scale influence.

Do not use patterns to blame teams. Use them to understand where the system needs support.

## Failure modes

These are common ways this skill goes wrong in practice:

- **Treating the total as the answer.** A team scores 75 and thinks they know where they stand — but two 75s can have completely different patterns. The number is a summary; the pattern is the diagnosis.
- **Fixing the lowest score by default.** "Scaling Influence is lowest, let's fix it first" — but the low score might be caused by a different weak dimension upstream. Read the *flow* before picking the fix.
- **Ignoring a weak link that's creating drag.** A balanced 80-80-80-80-50 looks healthy from a distance, but the 50 is breaking the whole system. Patterns make the weak link visible; treating it as "one outlier" misses the point.
- **Naming a pattern that doesn't match the data.** Reaching for "strong proof weak influence" because it sounds right rather than because Building Proof and Scaling Influence scores actually support it. Anchor the pattern call to the specific dimension shape.

## Note on terminology

The patterns above reference the five Design Assessment dimensions. None of those dimensions use the Decision Map's Define / Measure / Focus / Lead naming. (The Decision Map's "Show" phase was renamed to "Lead" in v1.3, but the assessment's dimensions and pattern types were unaffected.)
