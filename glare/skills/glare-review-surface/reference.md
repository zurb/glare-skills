# Surface Challenges — Reference

Compressed source: Glare | Design Review, Surface Techniques v1.3 + the S row of the Rubric and Scoring Model.

## Intent

Expose the real tension behind the request. Move the conversation from describing what is wanted to describing what is happening.

## When to use

- When the user is exposing the real tension behind a request in a design review.
- When the team is reacting to the design before agreeing on the problem.
- When feedback is scattered, the goal of the work is unclear, or no one named what is actually breaking.
- When Surface scored 1–2 on the SIGNAL rubric.
- When prepping a kickoff or any review where the problem is fuzzy.
- When the conversation jumped to solutions before naming consequence.

**Don't use when** the problem is already clear and the team needs success criteria (`glare-review-identify`) or when the team is debating opinion despite knowing the problem (`glare-review-ground`).

## Why it matters

Most requests are symptoms. If we optimize the surface, we create polished irrelevance. When the real challenge is visible, priorities clarify and influence increases.

When Surface is weak:
- feedback becomes scattered
- people react to details instead of what matters
- the same questions come up later
- decisions feel weak or get revisited

When Surface is strong, feedback gets sharper, decisions form faster, and the team has something real to respond to.

## What it considers

- whether the root issue is explored
- whether consequences are made visible
- whether the challenge is clearly acknowledged

## What to listen for

- language that describes friction or failure
- signs of urgency or pressure
- movement from requests to real situations

## What this unlocks

A clear challenge gives the conversation direction. It creates something real for the group to respond to.

## What breaks if it's weak

The conversation stays general. Ideas are discussed without being tied to a real problem.

## Connection to the next step

If the problem isn't clear, outcomes are harder to define.

## Scoring (1–5)

| Score | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Leading | Clear pain and consequence surfaced |
| 4 | Advancing | Tension identified, consequence partially quantified |
| 3 | Functional | Friction visible but soft — no consequence articulated |
| 2 | Developing | Surface-level acknowledgment, no depth |
| 1 | Reactive | Feature-level conversation only |

**Metric hint:** Use **Problem Ownership** — are clients/teammates acknowledging and naming tension?

## When to use Surface techniques

- people jump straight into opinions
- feedback spreads in different directions
- the goal of the work is unclear
- no one has named what is actually breaking
- the room is discussing the design before agreeing on the problem

By the end of Surface, the team should be able to say: what is not working, who it affects, why it matters now, what consequence to avoid.

## Technique chooser

| If… | Use… |
|---|---|
| The room lacks background | Set context before feedback begins |
| People are reacting to the design | Reframe the request into the problem |
| The issue feels vague | Ask what is not working today |
| Urgency is low | Make the consequence visible |

## The four techniques

### 1. Set context before feedback begins

When people see work for the first time in a meeting, they react to what's in front of them instead of what it's trying to solve. They don't know the background, what decisions were already made, or where uncertainty still exists. Feedback stays shallow or unfocused.

Setting context gives the room a shared starting point.

- **Watch for:** confusion or basic questions late in the discussion; feedback focused on small details; slow or hesitant start.
- **What it does:** creates shared understanding; improves the quality of feedback; helps the conversation start at the right level.
- **Example:** "Here's what we explored, why we made these choices, and where we're still unsure."

### 2. Reframe the request into the problem

If a conversation starts with the design itself, people interpret it in different ways. Feedback spreads in multiple directions because there is no shared understanding of what the work is solving.

Reframing brings the group back to the problem.

- **Watch for:** feedback pulling in different directions; people reacting based on preference; lack of clarity on the goal.
- **What it does:** aligns the room; focuses feedback; reduces noise.
- **Example:** "Before we react to this, we're trying to reduce drop-off at this step."

### 3. Ask what is not working today

Teams often avoid naming what is actually broken. The conversation stays polite, but it also stays shallow. Asking directly about what is not working brings real issues into the discussion.

- **Watch for:** vague statements about improvement; no clear problem being described; low urgency in the room.
- **What it does:** surfaces real friction; creates urgency; grounds the conversation.
- **Example:** "Where is this breaking down right now?"

### 4. Make the consequence visible

A problem without consequence rarely creates urgency. Without urgency, feedback feels optional and decisions take longer.

- **Watch for:** low energy or passive agreement; slow decision-making; unclear importance of the issue.
- **What it does:** raises the stakes; focuses attention; encourages action.
- **Example:** "If this doesn't improve, what happens?"

## Coaching for low Surface scores

**Common failure modes:**
- Accepting the stated request at face value ("they said redesign, so we're redesigning")
- Moving to solutions before understanding the problem
- Letting the client/PM set the frame instead of reframing

**Behavioral drills:**
- "Before we talk about what to build, help me understand what's not working today."
- "What happens if we don't address this in the next 90 days?"
- "When you say [their word], what does that actually look like?"

**Success signal:** Someone in the room articulates consequence or cost unprompted.

## Bottom line

Start with the problem, not the work. When the challenge is clear, feedback improves, decisions form faster, and the conversation moves forward with more confidence.

## Failure modes

These are common ways this skill goes wrong in practice:

- **Accepting the stated request at face value.** "They said redesign, so we're redesigning." The request is usually a symptom; Surface means naming what's actually breaking underneath.
- **Moving to solutions before understanding the problem.** The room jumps to "what if we…" within the first five minutes. Hold the line until the problem is named.
- **Letting the client or PM set the frame.** Treating the requester's framing as the truth. Reframing is part of Surface — "before we react to this, what's not working today?"
- **Naming the problem without naming the consequence.** "Onboarding is confusing" doesn't create urgency. "Onboarding is confusing AND we're seeing a 40% drop in week-one retention" does. Both halves matter.
