# Define — Compressed

## What this block is about

Define is the first (and foundational) facet of the Glare v1.0 framework — the "Credibility" facet. Its job is to slow teams down just long enough to agree on what matters before they start building. Define answers four questions: (1) What do users actually need? (2) Who are we designing for? (3) Where will the evidence come from? (4) How will we know it worked? It does this through four building blocks — **User Needs, Audience, Collecting Data, and UX Metrics** — that turn opinions, hunches, and vanity dashboards into testable signals. The recurring move across every doc is the same: replace opinion debates with structured signals tied to real users; balance attitudinal/behavioral/performance data; balance leading and lagging indicators; balance internal voices (team, stakeholders) with external voices (participants, customers); and tie every metric back to a UX outcome leaders can act on. Helio is the named in-house tool that lets these signals get captured in hours rather than months.

## Glare | Define Overview v1.1 (and v1.0)

- **Define = the Credibility facet of Glare.** It is "the first light that cuts through the Fog" of indecision; everything in Measure, Compare, and Lead later builds on what gets defined here.
- **Four building blocks** with a one-line core question for each:
  - **User Needs** — "What do users actually need or hope to achieve?" Anchors design in real problems, not assumptions.
  - **Audience** — "Who are we really designing for?" Ensures empathy and precision; keeps data relevant.
  - **Collecting Data** — "Where does clarity come from?" Turns hunches into hard evidence early.
  - **UX Metrics** — "How do we know it worked?" Gives design credibility through visible proof.
- **Seven UX Honeycomb layers (foundational → aspirational):** Findable, Accessible, Usable, Credible, Reliable, Valuable, Desirable. Each layer earns the right to the next; higher layers can't rescue a broken lower one.
- **Audience principle:** "Design for everyone and you design for no one." A marketer tracks bounce rate; a PM tracks adoption — same product, different signals.
- **Collecting principle:** Skipping collection only delays clarity. One small signal saves weeks. Cited proof: a university navigation test produced a 41% increase in positive impressions before launch.
- **UX Metrics principle:** Metrics are "the grammar of signals." A 70% completion rate can settle weeks of debate.
- **Quick checks per block:** Does this solve a real user goal? Can you name 3 audience traits? What real input are you using (survey/test/analytics)? Which metric proves progress (completion, effort, satisfaction)?
- **Without Define:** needs are guesses, audiences vague, data missing, metrics hollow. **With Define:** hunches become signals, ideas become user-backed decisions.

## Glare | Define, User Needs v1.1 (and v1.0)

- **Anchor design in what actually matters.** Before usability, metrics, or roadmaps, there is the moment a user decides your product matters to them — that moment is built on real needs, not assumptions.
- **Seven UX Honeycomb layers** (each layer earns the right to the next; foundational → aspirational):
  1. **Findable** — can users locate it? (comprehension, success rate, first-click)
  2. **Accessible** — can everyone perceive, operate, and understand it? (WCAG conformance, error recovery, alt-text coverage)
  3. **Usable** — can they act on it without friction? (task completion, time on task, error rate)
  4. **Credible** — do they believe it? (trust score, credibility ratings, perceived authority)
  5. **Reliable** — does it hold up every time? (completion under load, uptime, error recovery rate)
  6. **Valuable** — does it deliver an outcome worth the effort? (retention, intent-to-reuse, NPS)
  7. **Desirable** — does it pull them back emotionally? (satisfaction, desirability, sentiment)
- **Needfinding (Stanford-born):** uncovers requirements users can't articulate. Practice: (1) distinguish needs from wants ("faster horse" → "faster travel"); (2) observe before you ask; (3) use multiple sources (interviews, surveys, behavioral tests); (4) capture contradictions where words and behavior diverge.
- **Tie each need to a metric type** (per the 7-layer Honeycomb above): Findable/Accessible/Usable → completion, first-click, time-on-task, error rate; Credible/Reliable → trust score, completion under load, error recovery; Valuable → retention, intent-to-reuse, NPS; Desirable → satisfaction, desirability, sentiment.
- **Validation rule:** if users say they need it but ignore it in practice, it's a *want masquerading as a need*.
- **Traps:** vague goals like "make it better"; ignoring emotions; treating needs as static.
- **Proof in practice (university enrollment):** Hamburger menu lifted positive impressions 41% and usability 14% — the unmet need was *findability*.
- **Quick checklist:** list 3–5 user needs → match each to a metric → run a test → share the evidence → revisit.

## Glare | Define, Audience v1.1 (and v1.0)

- **Audience defines who your signals come from and how much weight each voice should carry.** Without this, customer data, stakeholder opinion, and personal preference all get weighted equally and metrics lose meaning.
- **Four primary audiences:**
  1. **Project Team** — provides *intent* (what we think will work). Light but continuous signal weight.
  2. **Stakeholders** — provide *direction* (what matters most to the business). Medium-to-high weight.
  3. **Customers** — provide *proof* (how it works in real life). Highest weight.
  4. **Participants** — provide *clarity* (how it feels before release). High during testing cycles.
- **Internal voices guide; external voices validate.** Customers confirm what works; participants predict what will.
- **Five core attribute types** (the texture beyond labels): Behavioral, Motivational, Contextual, Lifecycle, Demographic. Use 3–5 attributes total — enough to separate noise from signal.
- **v1.0 attribute taxonomy** (slightly different framing): Targeting, Demographics, Geographics, Psychographics, Behavior.
- **How to define an external audience (6 steps):** start with a question → identify customer vs participant → choose 3–5 attributes → map each to UX metrics → collect and compare → check whether participant signals predict customer behavior.
- **Balance trap:** too much participant data = confident in theory, fragile in practice; too much customer data = solid but slow to iterate.
- **Traps:** designing for "everyone"; over-segmenting until samples are too small; assuming you already know.
- **Proof in practice:** a "customers" group gave contradictory feedback; splitting into *new paying users* (needed trust/reassurance) and *habitual users* (needed speed/control) made signals align and decisions obvious.
- **The flow:** Project Teams form intent → Stakeholders align impact → Participants validate direction → Customers prove outcomes.

## Glare | Define, Audience, Project Team v1.1

- **Project teams are the first audience in Glare** — designers, researchers, engineers translating vision into early versions. Their assumptions are starting signals that need to be made visible and tested.
- **Why it matters:** every prototype/test plan/script carries team bias (how the problem is framed, what success looks like, what feels intuitive). Capturing intent before testing creates a benchmark you can compare belief vs behavior against.
- **Try this:** before each test, have the team list three predictions; review them side by side with results — gaps are where learning lives.
- **Five internal signal types:**
  - **Intent Clarity** — kickoff sessions, design briefs → Alignment, Understanding
  - **Decision Context** — assumption mapping → Comprehension, Traceability
  - **Design Confidence** — confidence survey, pre-test poll → Confidence, Readiness
  - **Iteration Learning** — retros, iteration logs → Speed, Effectiveness
  - **Cross-Discipline Alignment** — team check-ins, alignment maps → Efficiency, Collaboration
- **5-step process:** document intent (one sentence per sprint) → list 3–5 assumptions → assign one metric per assumption → compare to results → share both wins and surprises.
- **Common traps:** Silent Assumptions ("we already know"); Overconfidence (mistaking internal alignment for user understanding); Rush to Output; Disconnected Roles (designers, PMs, engineers defining success differently).
- **Audience build sequence:** Project Teams form intent → Stakeholders align impact → Customers prove outcomes → Participants validate direction.

## Glare | Define, Audience, Stakeholders v1.1

- **Stakeholders = the alignment audience.** They bring influence, resources, and expectations and decide which ideas move forward. Internal (executives, sponsors, program leaders) focus on business outcomes/risk/strategy; External (partners, distributors, agencies, investors, board, analysts) focus on market reach, financials, credibility.
- **Three stakeholder signal types:**
  - **Trust & Confidence** — interviews, short surveys → Confidence Score, Alignment Rating
  - **Clarity of Value** — Glare assessments, review discussions → Comprehension, Satisfaction
  - **Business Readiness** — roadmap checkpoints, exec briefings → Adoption, Momentum, Support Rate
- **Connect design to business workflows** (mapping table):
  - Sales → Revenue/conversion/pipeline → tie usability and clarity to close rates
  - Marketing → Engagement/brand trust → tie satisfaction/sentiment to campaign success
  - Product → Adoption/retention/NPS → tie completion/effort to product outcomes
  - Engineering → Efficiency/stability → use task clarity, time-on-task to validate quality
  - Strategy & Ops → Alignment/process → map design research to org readiness
  - Finance & Legal → Risk/compliance/cost → use trust and comprehension to de-risk change
- **5-step capture process:** Map influencers → Understand workflows → Link metrics to value → Run alignment checks → Show trends, not just results.
- **Traps:** speaking only design language ("delight," "beauty"); skipping business context; ignoring external influence; over-promising signals beyond measurable outcomes.

## Glare | Define, Audience, Customers v1.1

- **Customers = the proof audience.** Their behavior tells the truth internal discussions can't. While teams predict and participants validate, customers *confirm*.
- **Eight customer lifecycle segments** with what to measure for each:
  - **Trial / Pilot Accounts** — first impressions, onboarding clarity → Comprehension, Time on Task, Activation
  - **Freemium Users** — conversion, motivation to upgrade → Completion, Satisfaction, Trust
  - **New Paying Users** — setup success, perceived effort → Completion Rate, Confidence, Error Rate
  - **Habitual Users** — efficiency, reliability → Task Success, Retention, Usability
  - **Power Users** — depth of use, customization → Efficiency, Feature Use, Satisfaction
  - **Advocates** — emotional connection → Sentiment, NPS, Referral Rate
  - **Dormant Accounts** — drop-off causes, reactivation → Frequency, Reactivation Rate, Trust
  - **Churned Accounts** — barriers, exit drivers → Churn Rate, Sentiment, Exit Feedback
- **Five attribute categories** (with what each is best at measuring):
  - **Behavioral** (frequency, tenure, feature adoption, channel, task orientation) → completion, effort, time on task
  - **Motivational** (motivation, risk tolerance, value orientation, decision style, confidence) → satisfaction, trust, sentiment
  - **Contextual** (device, environment, connectivity, workload, collaboration) → error rate, time on task, clarity
  - **Lifecycle** (stage, tenure, activation, frequency, renewal cycle) → retention, activation, loyalty
  - **Demographic** (role, industry, region, age, education) → comprehension, accessibility, inclusivity
- **Use 3–5 attributes** that align with the research goal — attributes structure signals, not describe people.
- **Four metric lenses for customer signals:** by Time (leading vs lagging), by Stage (lifecycle phase), by Type (attitudinal/behavioral/performance), by Engagement (low/medium/high).
- **6-step define-and-measure flow:** start with a question → identify audience type → select key attributes → map attributes to metrics → collect and compare → close the loop (do participant signals predict customer behavior?).

## Glare | Define, Audience, Participants v1.1

- **Participants = the validation audience.** They are the early signal — proxy for reality, helping teams learn faster, spot friction earlier, and replace assumption with evidence before customers see it.
- **Helio context:** over a million participants worldwide. Lesson learned: people are messy and don't fit neat labels — describe by *attribute* (what they do) not by *title*.
- **Three essential questions every participant test answers:** (1) Do people understand what this design is trying to do? (2) Can they complete the main task easily? (3) Does it feel trustworthy enough to continue using?
- **Try this:** invite five adjacent users to complete the main task; track Completion, Confidence, Time on Task; see clarity gaps within an hour.
- **Four phases of participant testing:**
  - **Exploration** — concept/preference testing → attitudinal signals (reactions, mental models)
  - **Definition** — prototype/usability testing → behavioral signals (clarity, flow)
  - **Refinement** — A/B or sequential studies → performance signals (efficiency, trust)
  - **Launch Prep** — benchmark/pilot testing → combined signals (confidence, satisfaction)
- **Four types of participants:** Target Users, Adjacent Users, Informed Outsiders, Internal Proxies. You don't need demographic perfection — you need functional perspectives.
- **Five attribute types** (same taxonomy as Customers, with deeper sub-categories): Behavioral, Motivational, Contextual, Lifecycle, Demographic. Each attribute type maps to a primary signal and a best early metric (e.g., Behavioral → action pattern → Completion Rate; Motivational → emotional driver → Satisfaction; Contextual → environment → Error Rate; Lifecycle → familiarity over time → Retention; Demographic → inclusion/access → Comprehension).
- **6-step study structure:** Start with Purpose → Select 3–5 Attributes → Segment the Group → Run Small Iterative Tests (~100 per round) → Map Results to Metrics → Visualize Insights.
- **Three signal-collection methods:** Attitudinal (surveys, post-task) → Trust/Satisfaction/Comprehension; Behavioral (recordings, heatmaps) → Completion/Effort/Error Rate; Performance (timed tasks, benchmarks) → Time on Task/Success Rate.

## Glare | Define, Collecting v1.1 (and v1.0)

- **Collecting = turn raw input into usable signals.** It is the discipline that prevents drift back into "loudest voice wins."
- **5-section process structure:** Getting Started → Begin Collecting Data → Make It Practical → See It in Action → Apply and Evolve.
- **Cadence pattern across all "-ing" pages:** Define → Capture → Connect.
- **Four feedback types** (use to balance quantitative and qualitative):
  - **View user data** — analytics, clickstream, heatmaps
  - **See what users do** — task success, first-click, tree testing, time on task
  - **Sense what users like** — desirability, emotion tagging, satisfaction surveys
  - **Hear what users say** — surveys, interviews, in-product prompts, video feedback
- **Five-step collection process:**
  1. **Start with Intent** — pair user needs with business goals to create the tension that determines what data matters. Example hypothesis: "Users abandon checkout due to unclear pricing"; goal: "Reduce abandonment by 15% this quarter."
  2. **Choose Your Stack** — Research Stacks are reference; Design Stacks are practical. Three modes: Exploratory ("What should we be solving?"), Evaluative ("Does this design work?"), Comparative ("Which performs better?"). Five Design Stacks: Website, Mobile App, Product, E-commerce, Marketing.
  3. **Identify the Approach** — confirm question reveals a knowledge gap, points to observable behavior, and is measurable. Map to Exploring, Evaluating, or Comparing.
  4. **Apply the Techniques** — match to UX metrics: Exploratory (interviews, journey maps, diary studies, card sorting, surveys → usefulness, satisfaction, effort, trust); Evaluative (first-click, task success, time on task, heatmaps, clickstream, usability tests → completion, comprehension, efficiency, usability); Comparative (A/B, preference, multivariate, conversion, web analytics, eye tracking → conversion, engagement, desirability, confidence).
  5. **Connect and ready your data** — share at three levels: project (in the same tool), cross-team (Findings or Glare workspace by concept/metric), leadership (roadmap reviews/KPI). Always cite techniques + audience + metrics in any shared finding.
- **Build a consistent UX Metric Stack:** Behavioral (Completion, Time on Task, Error Rate); Attitudinal (Satisfaction, Trust, Desirability); Performance (Comprehension, Usability, Efficiency).
- **Transitions out:** Collecting → Measuring (calibrated benchmarks) → Compare (validate signals against outcomes). A "Design Signal" is the moment data starts to make sense — evidence becomes direction.
- **Practical worksheet:** Define Stack → Choose Technique → Select Tools → Identify Audience → Link to UX Metrics.
- **Proof in practice:** university navigation case — Evaluative stack + preference test + Helio = 14% usability lift in hours; signals beat opinions.

## Glare | Define, Collecting, Research Stacks v1.0

- **Research stacks turn familiar methods (NPS, SUS, A/B) into UX-metric-aligned signals** instead of isolated scores.
- **Three stack types:**
  - **Exploratory Stacks** — surface needs/opportunities → attitudinal metrics (satisfaction, usefulness). Examples: interviews, diary studies, journey mapping.
  - **Evaluative Stacks** — test if a design works → behavioral and performance metrics (task success, time on task, usability). Examples: SUS, first-click testing.
  - **Comparative Stacks** — show what works better → outcome metrics (preference, comprehension, engagement). Examples: preference tests, benchmarking, A/B testing.
- **Common research stacks (named instruments):**
  - **Usability & Ease:** SUS (System Usability Scale → usability/engagement/usefulness); SEQ (Single Ease Question → completion/success/satisfaction); PURE (expert-rated difficulty → completion/satisfaction/feeling); SUMI (efficiency/control → completion/usability/satisfaction)
  - **Effort & Workload:** CES (Customer Effort Score → completion/effort); CASTLE (task load/efficiency → task load/efficiency/usability/comprehension); NASA-TLX (workload → usability/usefulness/feeling/satisfaction)
  - **Satisfaction & Experience:** WAMMI (website satisfaction → appeal/usability/helpfulness/comprehension); PSSUQ (post-task satisfaction → engagement/usability/usefulness/feeling); QUIS (interface satisfaction); UEQ (emotional experience → usability/sentiment/reaction/loyalty); UX-Lite (quick UX snapshot → usefulness); SUPR-Q (website benchmark → usability/loyalty/sentiment/reaction)
  - **Trust & Loyalty:** NPS (likelihood to recommend → loyalty); L-DERLY (learnability/expectation gap → expectations/usability/usefulness/comprehension)
- **5-step usage:** start with the decision (explore/validate/compare) → choose matching stack → map methods to Glare metrics → pair stacks when needed → share results as signals tied to metrics leaders trust.
- **Traps:** treating methods in isolation; overusing one stack (interviews can't validate usability; A/B can't uncover unmet needs); skipping alignment.
- **Proof in practice:** retail team ran SUS aligned to usability/task success; SUS dropped below 50; redesign became urgent because the data was undeniable.

## Glare | Define, Collecting, Techniques v1.0

- **Techniques = repeatable research methods anchored to UX metrics.** They sit beneath stacks and tools as the tactical layer.
- **Five categories with examples → metrics tracked:**
  - **Navigation:** Card Sorting, Tree Testing → comprehension, expectations
  - **Task:** Task Success, Time on Task, SEQ → completion, effort, efficiency
  - **Comparison:** A/B Testing, Preference, Multivariate → conversion, desirability, comprehension
  - **Behavior:** Heatmaps, Clickstream, Web Analytics → attention, engagement, frequency
  - **Feedback:** Surveys, Questionnaires, Eye Tracking → satisfaction, sentiment, loyalty
- **Master technique table (verbatim mapping):**
  - First Click Testing → Success Rate, Comprehension, Desirability
  - Task Success Rate → Completion, Comprehension, Drop-Off
  - Time on Task → Efficiency, Success Rate
  - A/B Testing → Conversion Rate, Bounce Rate, Engagement
  - Multivariate Testing → CTR, Comprehension, Desirability
  - Heatmaps → Desirability, CTR, Comprehension
  - Clickstream Analysis → Drop-Off, Efficiency, Frequency
  - Web Analytics → Session Duration, Bounce Rate, Conversion Rate
  - Conversion Rate Analysis → Success Rate, Drop-Off, Desirability
  - Eye Tracking → Comprehension, Efficiency, Visual Attention
  - Card Sorting → Comprehension, Expectations
  - Surveys & Questionnaires → Usefulness, Satisfaction, Loyalty
  - Quantitative Template → custom mapping
- **5-step usage:** pick the metric → choose the technique → run lean (small samples) → pair techniques (Task Success + Time on Task) → frame results as signals not raw output.
- **Traps:** running techniques without metrics ("a card sort is just boxes" without a comprehension/usability target); overcomplicating; one-and-done.
- **Proof in practice:** university first-click test (Helio) → 14% comprehension lift in hours, ending weeks of debate.

## Glare | Define, Collecting, Tools v1.0

- **Start with the metric, not the tool.** Tools are infrastructure for capturing UX metrics; the wrong tool buries teams in noise.
- **Four-axis framework for tool selection:** View user data (analytics) → See what users do (recordings, heatmaps) → Sense what users like (eye tracking, appeal) → Hear what users say (surveys, in-product feedback).
- **Four tool categories (with named platforms):**
  - **Attitudinal Tools:** Surveys (Typeform, SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics); In-product feedback (Sprig, Delighted, Qualaroo); Preference testing (UsabilityHub, Helio); Benchmarking (NPS, CES, CSAT) → satisfaction, trust, desirability, sentiment, brand score
  - **Behavioral Tools:** Analytics (Google Analytics, Mixpanel, Amplitude); Session replay & heatmaps (Hotjar, FullStory, CrazyEgg, Smartlook); Clickstream (Pendo, Heap); Navigation testing (Treejack, Optimal Workshop) → completion, engagement, navigation paths, usability
  - **Performance Tools:** Usability platforms (UserTesting, Maze, UserZoom); A/B & multivariate (Optimizely, VWO, Google Optimize); Task timing & error (Helio, Lookback); System monitoring (Pingdom, New Relic, Load Impact) → time on task, error rate, drop-off, task success
  - **Specialized Tools:** Eye tracking (Tobii, RealEye); Biometrics & emotion (iMotions, Affectiva); Privacy-first analytics (Fathom, Plausible); Emerging (AR/VR usability, AI-driven sentiment) → attention, emotional response, advanced trust/performance
- **4-step usage:** start with the metric → match the tool → pair tools (survey + heatmap to see what users say *and* where they click) → stay lean.
- **Traps:** collecting everything; tool sprawl; over-relying on one type (e.g., analytics without attitudinal).
- **Proof in practice:** fintech tracked onboarding with analytics only; saw 70% completion and assumed success. Pairing with Helio surveys revealed users felt confused even while finishing — the fix came from combining tools.

## Glare | Define, UX Metrics v1.0

- **A UX metric quantifies how users feel and behave** — turning satisfaction, comprehension, and usability into trackable signals.
- **Three types (the foundational taxonomy):**
  - **Attitudinal** (how users feel): Brand Score, Desirability, Expectations, Feeling, Helpfulness, Trust, Loyalty, Satisfaction, Reaction, Sentiment, Usefulness
  - **Behavioral** (what users do): Completion, Comprehension, Effort, Engagement, Frequency, Intent, Success, Usability
  - **Performance** (efficiency/accuracy): split into Marketing (Avg Time on Page, Avg Session Duration, Pages per Session, Bounce Rate, Exit Rate, Device Type, Event Tracking), eCommerce (Engagement, Abandonment Rate, Bounce Rate, Store Sessions), and Product (Stickiness, Activation Rate, Feature Usage, Time to Activate, Retention Rate, Click-back Rate)
- **4-step discipline:** Start small (1–2 metrics tied to a real issue) → Test and learn → Share and align → Adjust as you grow.
- **Four principles for choosing metrics** (each metric must pass these tests):
  - **Easy to Understand** — explainable in one sentence; otherwise it won't guide decisions
  - **Good for Comparison** — patterns over time, not single snapshots ("432 clicks" is trivia; "27% comprehension lift" is proof)
  - **Use Rates or Ratios** — raw counts are vanity (10k visits with 98% bounce)
  - **Focus on Behavior** — what people do beats what they say
- **Four critical comparisons:** Vanity vs Actionable; Qualitative vs Quantitative; Exploratory vs Reporting; Correlated vs Causal (the "Monday usage spike" example: caused by support emails, not the feature).
- **UX Metrics in Practice — four lenses (each its own sub-doc):**
  - **By Type** (attitudinal / behavioral / performance)
  - **By Stage** (predictive / proxy / analytics)
  - **By Time** (leading / lagging)
  - **By Engagement** (needs / behavior / activity)
- **Quick checklist:** can it be explained in one sentence; comparable; a rate not a count; behavior-grounded; actionable; pairs *what* with *why*; fits timing; balances leading/lagging.

## Glare | Define, UX Metrics, By Type v1.0

- **Three lenses every product can be measured by:** how users feel (attitudinal), what they do (behavioral), how well they can do it (performance). Ignore one and the picture distorts.
- **Attitudinal Metrics:** Do they trust this? Are they happy? Will they return? Examples: Brand Score, Desirability, Satisfaction.
- **Behavioral Metrics:** Are they finishing? Do they engage deeply? Where do they drop off? Examples: Completion Rate, Engagement, Usability.
- **Performance Metrics:** Is it efficient? Where stuck? How smoothly? Examples: Time on Task, Error Rate, Drop-off Rate.
- **Diagnostic mismatches (key insight):**
  - High satisfaction + low completion = people like the idea but can't finish.
  - Strong performance + low engagement = system works but no one cares.
  - High completion + low satisfaction = users grind through; warning sign for churn.
- **5-step method:** define the question (perception/action/efficiency) → pick one metric from each type (a trio, not ten) → run a small test → look for mismatches → share the trio together.
- **Traps:** relying on one type; collecting too many signals; treating metrics as static.
- **Proof in practice:** team saw 80% signup completion (looked great), then added satisfaction (most users confused) and time on task (slow/painful) — redesigned for clarity, got faster signups and lasting retention.

## Glare | Define, UX Metrics, By Time v1.0

- **Anticipate Early, Prove Later.** Most teams overweight lagging data because executives recognize it; lagging tells you what already happened when damage is done.
- **Leading Indicators** — forecast what's likely to happen. Are users interested? Do they understand? Will this block them later? Examples: clicks/sign-ups during early exposure, prototype usage, survey comprehension, beta feedback.
- **Lagging Indicators** — confirm what happened after launch. Did adoption hold? Did retention grow? Examples: customer satisfaction, revenue, conversion rates, post-launch task success, retention over time.
- **Why pair them:** leading-only = chasing noise; lagging-only = learning too late. Leading sparks adjustments before launch; lagging proves those adjustments created value.
- **4-step method:** identify the window (anticipate vs evaluate) → pick matching signal → pair them deliberately → connect them (do leading signals correlate with long-term outcomes? recalibrate if not).
- **Traps:** relying only on lagging; over-trusting leading (early excitement ≠ loyalty); not linking the two.
- **Proof in practice:** SaaS collaboration feature had high desirability and beta usage (leading), but adoption plateaued and retention didn't improve (lagging). Mismatch revealed users liked the idea in theory but never built it into workflows; team redirected effort.

## Glare | Define, UX Metrics, By Stage v1.0

- **Three stages of metrics:** Predictive, Proxy, Analytics. A metric useful in testing is useless once live, and vice versa.
- **Predictive Metrics** — leading indicators forecasting outcomes by revealing confusion or friction before launch. Logic: "If A happens, B will result, and C should decline." Example: 70% of users confused by a landing page in a survey.
- **Proxy Metrics** — show how a design performs in controlled settings (usability tests, concept evals). Logic: "If B happens, C will decline." Example: 50% can complete a flow in test.
- **Analytics** — measure live behavior. Logic: "C is declining." Example: time spent in section + completion → design supports the goal. Analytics show *what* but rarely *why*.
- **4-step method:** identify the stage (exploring/testing/live) → pick matching metric → pair them → close the loop (feed analytics into next predictive tests).
- **Traps:** relying only on analytics; trusting proxies too much; forgetting the loop.
- **Proof in practice:** financial app saw 60% drop-off in old onboarding. Predictive tests on prototypes revealed users didn't understand key terms (refined language); proxy usability showed 90% task completion; analytics post-launch confirmed adoption rose, drop-off fell below 20%.

## Glare | Define, UX Metrics, By Engagement v1.0

- **Engagement is a progression, not a single number.** Confirm need → confirm use → confirm return. Skip a step and you chase illusions.
- **Three layers:**
  - **Needs-Based** (customer understanding) — does this solve a real problem; do users see value? Examples: brand score, desirability, usefulness, onboarding completion, power-user feature adoption.
  - **Behavior-Based** (customer behavior) — are users finishing actions; using features meaningfully? Examples: completion rate, navigation paths, usage frequency, engagement scores.
  - **Activity-Based** (customer activity) — do they keep coming back; how long; routine? Examples: repeat visits, session duration, drop-off rate, recency of use.
- **Diagnostic mismatches:**
  - High desirability + low usage = people love the idea but won't adopt.
  - Strong usage + weak loyalty = try once and move on.
  - Consistent activity + low value = trapped in habit, no real outcome.
- **4-step method:** start with needs → layer in behavior → add activity → look for gaps (the lagging layer is your friction point).
- **Traps:** chasing shallow numbers (clicks/time on page); skipping layers (adoption without loyalty = delayed churn); mistaking activity for value.
- **Proof in practice:** productivity tool's collaboration feature had high desirability + promising one-time usage, but repeat use dropped within weeks — excitement was real, loyalty missing.

## Key terms

- **Glare** — the overall framework. Define is its first facet ("Credibility"). Other facets named in passing: Measure, Compare, Lead, Show, Focus.
- **Fog** — the state of indecision Glare is designed to cut through.
- **Define** — the credibility-building first facet. Four blocks: User Needs, Audience, Collecting, UX Metrics.
- **Signal** — a measurable, trustworthy piece of evidence (vs raw "data"). The unit of credibility in Glare.
- **Design Signal** — what data becomes when patterns make sense; evidence turning into direction.
- **Helio** — the in-house testing platform referenced throughout (used for first-click tests, preference tests, surveys, usability tests; gives signals in hours not weeks; 1M+ participants run through it).
- **Needfinding** — Stanford-born practice for surfacing real (not stated) needs.
- **The Seven UX Honeycomb Layers** — Findable, Accessible, Usable, Credible, Reliable, Valuable, Desirable (foundational → aspirational; higher layers can't compensate for a broken lower layer).
- **Four Audiences** — Project Team (intent), Stakeholders (alignment), Participants (clarity/validation), Customers (proof).
- **Five Attribute Types** — Behavioral, Motivational, Contextual, Lifecycle, Demographic. Use 3–5 to define a testable group.
- **Three Modes of Learning** — Exploratory, Evaluative, Comparative.
- **Research Stacks vs Design Stacks** — Research = reference instruments (SUS, NPS, etc.); Design = practical layers where concepts get measured (Website, Mobile App, Product, E-commerce, Marketing).
- **Three UX Metric Types** — Attitudinal, Behavioral, Performance.
- **Four UX Metric Lenses** — By Type, By Stage (Predictive/Proxy/Analytics), By Time (Leading/Lagging), By Engagement (Needs/Behavior/Activity).
- **Customer Lifecycle Segments** — Trial, Freemium, New Paying, Habitual, Power, Advocates, Dormant, Churned.
- **Define → Capture → Connect** — the cadence pattern across all "-ing" pages in Glare.
- **Vanity vs Actionable / Qualitative vs Quantitative / Exploratory vs Reporting / Correlated vs Causal** — the four metric-comparison filters.
- **Four named research instruments worth knowing:** SUS, SEQ, NPS, CES (plus PURE, SUMI, CASTLE, NASA-TLX, WAMMI, PSSUQ, QUIS, UEQ, UX-Lite, SUPR-Q, L-DERLY).
