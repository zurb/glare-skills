# Decision Map — Compressed (v1.34, 2026-05-11)

## What the Decision Map is

The Decision Map is the **operational system** that connects user needs, design signals, decisions, and outcomes across product and design work. (v1.34 framing — earlier versions described it as a "map"; v1.34 makes the operational-system framing explicit.) Glare is built on four facets — **Define, Measure, Focus, Lead** — each playing a different role:
- **Define** clarifies what matters.
- **Measure** creates signals.
- **Focus** turns those signals into decisions.
- **Lead** connects those decisions to results.

The Decision Map brings these pieces together. It shows how a decision moves from a **user need** to a **measurable outcome** without getting lost between tools, teams, or conversations.

Modern product workflows move across **research, testing, AI systems, reviews, metrics, stakeholders, and business goals**. As work speeds up, teams often lose connection between those parts. The Decision Map helps teams keep direction connected as work moves through Define, Measure, Focus, and Lead.

A Decision Map is a way to follow a design decision from where it starts to what it changes. Most teams already have ideas, testing, reviews, and feedback. The problem is those pieces sit in different places: research in one doc, metrics in another tool, decisions in meetings, outcomes reported later. The map connects the chain.

It helps the team see:
- what user need started the work
- what signals were captured
- what the team learned
- what decision was made
- what outcome changed

Without that connection, work still moves forward, but the logic gets harder to follow. The map makes the path visible.

## Why it matters

Work loses strength as it moves forward:
- A problem is defined clearly at the start, but becomes vague later.
- Signals are captured, but not used in decisions.
- Metrics are tracked, but don't influence direction.
- Outcomes are discussed, but not tied back to the original problem.

From the outside, the work looks active. Inside, the thread breaks.

The Decision Map keeps the thread intact:
- what you define is what you measure
- what you measure is what you use to decide
- what you decide connects to an outcome
- what you learn feeds the next cycle

This is what turns design work from separate activities into a connected decision system.

## How decisions move in Glare

Every decision follows the same path. It starts with understanding the problem, moves through testing and learning, becomes a clear direction, and ends in measurable outcomes.

### Define — sets the foundation

Define is where work becomes grounded. The team is not solving anything yet — they are working to clearly understand what is happening and why it matters.

Define focuses on:
- identifying real user needs, not just requests
- understanding who the audience is and how they behave
- recognizing recurring **patterns** (situations that repeatedly produce the same behaviors and signals — added as the fifth Define building block in v1.34)
- clarifying the context around the problem
- agreeing on what success should look like

Define's five building blocks: **User Needs, Audience, Patterns, Collecting Data, UX Metrics.** Patterns was added in v1.34 because as AI accelerates output, teams need a faster way to recognize recurring situations before momentum scales a weak decision.

When Define is strong, the rest of the work has something real to respond to. When Define is weak, teams test ideas without a clear reason — work may move forward, but it becomes harder to know whether it is solving the right problem.

### Measure — creates evidence

Measure is where ideas meet reality. Once the problem is clear, the team starts testing possible ways to improve it. Instead of debating internally, they put ideas in front of users and observe what happens.

Measure works through:
- concepts that represent possible solutions
- hunches that need to be validated or challenged
- questions that uncover real behavior
- findings that show patterns in the data

This is where signals are created. Signals turn raw feedback into evidence the team can use — they show whether an idea makes sense, creates confidence, causes friction, or helps people move forward. Without Measure, decisions rely on assumptions.

### Focus — shapes direction

Focus is where the work narrows. The team has enough signal to stop exploring and start deciding. The goal shifts from learning to committing.

Focus does:
- uses the right methods to validate what matters
- tracks performance through design KPIs
- compares results across versions or benchmarks
- makes decisions based on what holds up

This is where teams get stuck without strong signals — Focus turns into debate. When earlier steps are clear, Focus becomes easier: the team can see tradeoffs, choose a path, and explain why that choice makes sense.

### Lead — connects to impact

Lead is where the decision proves its value. After a direction is chosen, the work doesn't stop. The team understands what actually changed and why it matters to the business.

Lead connects decisions to:
- business goals like growth, retention, or efficiency
- workflows across product, marketing, and other teams
- relationships between UX metrics and product KPIs
- results that can be tracked and explained

Without Lead, decisions feel isolated. A team may know the work improved, but cannot clearly show why it mattered. With Lead, the decision becomes part of a larger impact story.

## Use the Decision Map

The Decision Map is not just something to understand — it is something you run. The fastest way to start is by using the Skills (structured prompts and workflows) that guide you through each step.

You can use the map to:
- map signals to the UX metrics they influence
- connect those metrics to product-level changes
- link product changes to business outcomes
- trace a decision from the original problem to its impact

Each Skill focuses on a specific part of the map, but they work best together. Over time, they build a consistent way of making and evaluating decisions.

## Worked example: simplify mobile onboarding (v1.34 example)

**Decision:** Simplify a mobile onboarding flow. (v1.34 reframed the canonical example from mobile navigation to mobile onboarding; the chain is identical.)

- **Define** — Users are struggling to understand the next step after account setup. The team needs to understand which actions matter most and where people lose confidence.
- **Measure** — The team tests multiple onboarding directions using **comprehension, usability, task success, and confidence** metrics. Signals reveal where users hesitate and which flow creates stronger understanding.
- **Focus** — One onboarding direction consistently performs better across key metrics and creates less confusion. The team chooses that direction because the evidence holds up across user response and workflow performance.
- **Lead** — The onboarding decision connects to **activation, support reduction, onboarding completion, and product adoption.** The team can now explain why the decision matters beyond the interface itself.

This is the purpose of the Decision Map: connect the need, signal, decision, and result into one clear story.

## Connecting the system

The Decision Map sits across all four facets and connects them into a continuous flow.

A decision doesn't stop at any one step. It starts in **Define**, where user needs and problems are clarified. Those needs move into **Measure**, where they become testable ideas. What is learned produces signals that carry into **Focus**, where the team uses them to make a clear decision. That decision moves into **Lead**, where it becomes a visible outcome.

That outcome doesn't end the process. It feeds back into Define, shaping how the next problem is understood. This creates a **loop, not a straight line**. Each cycle makes decisions clearer because they're built on what was learned before.

## What the map makes visible

Instead of disconnected steps, the map shows the chain between:
- **signal** → **metric** → **decision** → **outcome**

This matters because teams often lose the connection between what they learned and what they chose:
- A finding may be useful, but if it doesn't shape the decision, it fades.
- A metric may be tracked, but if it doesn't connect to an outcome, it becomes reporting.
- A decision may be made, but if no one can explain the impact, it becomes hard to defend later.

The map keeps those pieces connected. It helps teams see what happened, why it happened, and what should happen next.

## What improves when the map is clear

- Signals don't get lost as work moves between groups. They continue to shape direction.
- Metrics become part of decision-making, not just reporting.
- Decisions feel grounded and easier to commit to. There is a clearer line from problem to outcome.
- Conversations move faster because the team builds on shared evidence instead of revisiting the same questions.
- Over time, this creates consistency — teams approach decisions in the same way, reducing friction.

## What breaks when it's missing

- Define and Measure drift apart. What gets tested no longer ties back to the original problem.
- Signals are collected but don't strongly influence decisions. Focus depends more on opinion.
- Lead becomes harder to prove. Outcomes may exist, but the path from decision to impact is unclear.
- The work still happens, but cause and effect become hard to connect. Hard to learn, hard to repeat what worked.

## Where the Decision Map fits in Glare

Each part of Glare supports a different part of the system:
- **Design Review** improves how decisions are made in conversations.
- **Design Signals** provide the evidence used in decisions.
- **Decision Map** connects decisions across the system (Define → Measure → Focus → Lead).
- **Design Assessment** shows where the system is strong or breaking.

The Decision Map keeps everything aligned as work moves forward. By keeping each step connected, it helps teams:
- make clearer decisions
- move faster with more confidence
- show how design work creates real impact

It turns separate activities into a system that works together.

## Cross-cutting concepts (referenced across multiple facets)

The Decision Map master doc surfaces several concepts that don't sit cleanly inside one facet — they live across the chain:

- **Signal → Metric → Decision → Outcome chain.** Cross-cuts every facet; should be referenced in Measure (signal), Focus (metric/decision), and Lead (outcome).
- **The loop back from Outcome to Define.** Lives between Lead and Define — the next cycle's Define is shaped by the last cycle's Lead.
- **What you define is what you measure / what you measure is what you decide on / what you decide connects to outcome.** The "thread" rule that holds the four facets together; belongs in the Decision Map parent and should be cross-referenced from each facet's parent skill.
- **"Work loses strength as it moves forward."** The failure mode the map exists to prevent — applies to all four facets.
- **Strategic vs tactical decisions.** Surfaces in Decisions (Focus) but also relevant to Lead, since strategic decisions often shape investment, resourcing, and roadmap.

## What changed in v1.34 (2026-05-11)

- **Opening framing tightened to "operational system."** Earlier versions described the Decision Map as something teams *use*; v1.34 makes the operational-system framing explicit — it is what connects user needs, design signals, decisions, and outcomes across product and design work.
- **Explicit AI mention in the modern-workflow context.** v1.34 lists "research, testing, AI systems, reviews, metrics, stakeholders, and business goals" as the parts the map holds together. AI is now named as one of the streams that fragments unless the map holds.
- **Patterns added to Define.** Inside the Define facet description, the v1.34 source surfaces a fifth building block: Patterns — recognizing recurring situations before weak decisions gain momentum. Patterns sits alongside User Needs, Audience, Collecting Data, and UX Metrics, with its own dedicated v1.0 source doc.
- **No structural shift to Measure / Focus / Lead.** The four-facet flow itself is unchanged; the loop back from Lead to Define is unchanged; the worked mobile-onboarding example is unchanged in shape (now framed around onboarding rather than mobile navigation, but the four-facet trace is the same chain).
