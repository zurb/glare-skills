# Guiding Decisions — Design Assessment Dimension

Compressed from "Glare | Design Assessment, Guiding Decisions v1.3" (May 3, 2026).

> "Design work gets weaker when teams cannot turn signals into clear choices."

## What this dimension measures

How well your team **uses evidence to move work forward**. Strong design teams do not just collect signals — they use them to clarify options, reduce debate, and commit to the next step. The dimension shows whether design evidence helps teams make decisions or gets lost in discussion.

A team strong in this dimension can clearly answer:

- What decision are we trying to make?
- What options are on the table?
- What signal would help us choose?
- What tradeoffs matter most?
- What evidence changed the direction?
- What choice did we make?
- What happens next because of that choice?

## Why it matters

Most teams have more input than they can act on. They hear from users, stakeholders, leadership, sales, support, research, analytics, the market. The problem is not that teams lack information — it is that information often does not turn into a decision.

When that happens:

- meetings circle without a clear choice
- evidence gets discussed but not used
- teams revisit the same decisions
- tradeoffs stay fuzzy
- stakeholders push different directions
- research becomes interesting but not decisive
- the loudest voice can still win

The work may have plenty of input, but the direction does not sharpen.

## Where momentum breaks

Momentum breaks when signals do not become choices. A test shows a clear pattern, but the team still debates the direction. A review surfaces useful feedback, but no one decides what changes. A stakeholder asks for more evidence, but the team is not clear what decision the evidence needs to support.

You see weak Guiding Decisions when:

- meetings end without a clear decision
- teams keep asking for more data without defining what would be enough
- findings are shared but not connected to tradeoffs
- options are compared loosely
- decisions depend on hierarchy instead of evidence
- the same issue comes back in later reviews
- teams leave with action items but no real commitment
- no one can explain why one path was chosen over another

The issue is not that teams are indecisive. It is that the **decision path is not clear enough to follow.**

## What strong looks like

Strong Guiding Decisions makes evidence useful in the moment. The team defines the decision, frames the options, uses signals to compare tradeoffs, and commits to a direction. Evidence does not sit beside the conversation. It shapes the conversation.

Strong teams:

- name the decision before debating solutions
- define what evidence would help
- compare options against clear criteria
- use signals to clarify tradeoffs
- separate opinion from evidence
- decide what to ship, refine, pause, or stop
- make ownership clear
- turn decisions into next actions

This does not mean every decision needs heavy research. It means the team knows how to use the **right signal at the right moment.**

## What weak looks like

Weak Guiding Decisions creates repeated debate. The team may have research, feedback, and data, but the path to a decision stays unclear. People discuss evidence without agreeing on what it means or what action it should create.

Weak teams often:

- start reviews without naming the decision
- debate solutions before agreeing on criteria
- collect signals that do not connect to a choice
- treat all feedback as equal
- avoid tradeoffs to keep everyone comfortable
- revisit decisions after they were supposedly made
- leave ownership unclear
- move forward without knowing why

This makes design feel subjective, even when evidence exists.

## How it is evaluated — the four decision areas

Guiding Decisions looks at the **type of decision** being made and whether the team is using the right kind of reasoning for that moment. Some decisions are still exploratory. Some need operational direction. Some need deeper analysis. Others need strong evidence before the team commits.

The assessment focuses on four decision areas:

```
Exploratory   |  Operational
Analytical    |  Evidential
```

These four areas sit across **two tensions**:

- **Effectual ↔ Causal:** whether the team starts from what it has or works backward from a defined goal
- **Abstract ↔ Certain:** whether the team is still shaping the idea or has enough evidence to commit

When these areas are clear, teams make better decisions with less friction. When they are blurred, teams over-plan early ideas, under-test risky choices, or keep exploring when it is time to commit.

### Area 1 — Exploratory

Exploratory decisions happen when the team is still **shaping the work**. Early, abstract work where the team has rough ideas, incomplete evidence, existing constraints, and available means. The goal is not to lock the answer — it is to turn early thinking into a direction the team can learn from.

- **Strong:** the team captures assumptions, names hunches, and makes the early direction clear enough to test.
- **Weak:** ideas stay fuzzy. Teams jump into solutions without making the underlying assumptions visible.

> Exploratory decisions need **shaping, not certainty.**

### Area 2 — Operational

Operational decisions happen when the team has a goal and **needs to organize the work around it**. Goal-directed work where the team knows the outcome it wants, but still needs to define the path, constraints, methods, and workflow.

- **Strong:** the team connects goals to methods, ownership, workflows, and success criteria.
- **Weak:** the team has a desired outcome, but the path stays vague. People are busy, but the work is not clearly organized around the goal.

> Operational decisions need **structure, not more loose discussion.**

### Area 3 — Analytical

Analytical decisions happen when the team is **using signals to understand what is changing**. Evidence-seeking work — collecting input, asking questions, comparing options, looking for patterns. The goal is to turn signals into clearer learning.

- **Strong:** the team uses UX metrics, findings, and comparisons to understand what changed and what needs to improve.
- **Weak:** signals are collected but not interpreted clearly. The team has data, but it does not know what the data should change.

> Analytical decisions need **interpretation, not more scattered input.**

### Area 4 — Evidential

Evidential decisions happen when the team has **enough proof to commit**. High-commitment work — choosing a direction, shipping a version, funding a bet, retiring an option, making a roadmap decision. The goal is to move forward with evidence strong enough to support the choice.

- **Strong:** the team can clearly explain what was chosen, what was rejected, why the choice matters, and what happens next.
- **Weak:** commitment stays fragile. Teams make decisions but keep reopening them because the rationale was not strong or visible enough.

> Evidential decisions need **commitment, not more circling.**

## How to strengthen Guiding Decisions

Start by naming the decision before debating the solution. You do not need a new meeting format or a heavy governance process. Start with one decision and make the path easier to follow.

The goal is to create a clearer decision trail:

```
Uncertainty → Options → Signal → Choice
```

That trail should show what decision needed to be made, what options were considered, what evidence mattered, and what choice moved the work forward.

**Useful moves:**

- name the decision before the discussion starts
- define the options clearly
- write down the tradeoffs
- identify what signal would help the team choose
- separate opinions from evidence
- compare options against the same criteria
- state what evidence changed the direction
- confirm the choice in plain language
- assign ownership for the next step

The goal is not decision-making theater. It is to make choices easier to understand, trust, and act on.

## Diagnostic questions

Use these to evaluate how well your team guides decisions:

- What decision are we trying to make?
- Are the options clear?
- What tradeoffs matter most?
- What evidence would be enough to move forward?
- What signal changed our thinking?
- Did we choose, refine, pause, or stop?
- Can someone explain why this path was chosen?
- Who owns the next step?

## What improves when this is strong

When Guiding Decisions is strong, design becomes easier to act on:

- teams spend less time circling
- reviews end with clearer direction
- research becomes more useful because it is tied to choices
- leaders trust the work because the reasoning is visible
- repeated debate reduces
- tradeoffs become explicit
- evidence gets used in the moment
- teams move from discussion to commitment
- ownership becomes clear
- decisions become easier to defend
- momentum survives after the review

This is where design maturity becomes easier to lead.

## How this dimension supports the others

When decisions are easier to guide, teams can:

- **organize work** around clearer choices
- **manage complexity** with stronger tradeoffs
- **build proof** from decisions that are easier to trace
- **scale influence** because leaders can see why the direction changed

## Smallest-leverage next move

Pick one recent decision. Trace it: Uncertainty → Options → Signal → Choice. Find the weakest link. Can someone see what decision needed to be made, what options were considered, what signal mattered, and why the team chose that path?

Even simpler: at the next decision conversation, name the decision before the team starts debating solutions. That single shift usually exposes whether the right kind of reasoning is being used.

Do **not** start by adopting a heavy decision-making framework. Start with one decision, named clearly.

## Note on terminology

The four areas (Exploratory, Operational, Analytical, Evidential) are organized by two tensions (Effectual ↔ Causal, Abstract ↔ Certain) — not by the Decision Map's Define / Measure / Focus / Lead phases. (The Decision Map's "Show" phase was renamed to "Lead" in v1.3.) The Decision Map describes the operating cycle of a project; Guiding Decisions describes the reasoning required at any given decision moment. They reinforce each other: the Lead phase is where Evidential decisions happen most often.
