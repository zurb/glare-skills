You are helping the user **capture Design Signals as part of everyday work** — not after the fact.

## Core idea

Signals are most useful when they are part of the work, not something added after. Many teams wait until the end to gather feedback or review metrics; by then decisions are made and direction is hard to change. Capturing signals **earlier** keeps the work grounded while it is still moving. The capture flow has four stages — **Frame → Ground → Measure → Use** — each with concrete moves that turn a small moment into a decision-grade signal.

## Read the reference first

- `reference.md` — full content for each of the four stages, the eight concrete moves inside them (start with a hunch → make it clear → connect to what matters → anchor to a metric → put it next to the work → bring in real input → compare and read → decide and keep attached), examples, what the workflow produces, and where it breaks.

## How to apply

1. **Anchor the user to a real moment.** Capture starts when something in a review or in the work doesn't feel right — not in a quarterly planning session. Ask: "What's the moment you're looking at right now?" Pull the hunch out of the conversation and **make it visible**.

2. **Walk the four stages in order, but lightly:**
   - **Frame** — start with a hunch, then make it clear by turning it into a specific question.
   - **Ground** — connect to what matters: the user need ("what the user is trying to accomplish") and the business goal ("the outcome the team is trying to move").
   - **Measure** — anchor to a metric that matches the question; put the signal next to real design options; bring in just enough user input (metrics + comments) to reduce uncertainty.
   - **Use** — compare and read the options against the signal; decide what moves forward; keep the signal attached so the decision holds across reviews and handoffs.

3. **Push for "make it visible" at every step.** The biggest failure mode in the doc is hunches that stay in conversation. Convert them into a written question, a named user need, a chosen metric, a side-by-side comparison, an attached rationale.

4. **Keep input lightweight.** This is not a research project. The doc is explicit: "the goal is not to run a full study, but to reduce uncertainty." Look for both metrics (what users did) and comments (why they reacted that way).

5. **Stay with the signal during decision-making.** Most teams "fall back into opinion" at the comparison stage. Force the conversation to stay on: where is one direction clearly stronger, where do both struggle, where do results and comments align.

6. **Keep the signal attached.** Most decisions don't fail in the moment — they drift later. The final move is preserving the rationale ("we chose this because users understood it faster") so the decision survives reviews, handoffs, and future changes.
