# Signal Components — Reference

Source: Glare | Design Signal, Components v1.3, May 3, 2026.

## Why this exists

Signals only work when the pieces come together. Many teams already have input — they see behavior, review metrics, talk through what feels off — but those pieces don't always connect. The result is more discussion, not more clarity. Components shows how to bring that input together so it can guide a decision.

## When to use

- When the user is building or strengthening an individual Design Signal.
- When asking "what goes into a signal" or "my signal feels incomplete."
- When walking through the six structural components — Design intuition, UX metric, User need, Context, Business goal, Direction.
- When debugging which piece is missing or weak in an existing signal.
- When turning a hunch into a usable signal by adding the missing components.

**Don't use when** the user is asking about the four signal types Need/Use/Prefer/Adopt (`glare-signals-types`), evaluating whether a signal is good enough to act on (`glare-signals-quality`), or the end-to-end capture workflow (`glare-signals-capturing`).

## What goes into a signal

Every signal starts with a mix of input:
- what users did or said
- how the experience performed
- what the team notices or questions

On their own, these don't move the work forward. They need structure.

## Structure of a Signal — the six parts

A complete signal connects six parts:

- **Design intuition** → what the team thinks might be happening
- **UX metric** → what users actually did or reported
- **User need** → what the user is trying to accomplish
- **Context** → where the interaction happens
- **Business goal** → what outcome matters
- **Direction** → what the team should do next

When these line up, the signal becomes usable. The team can see what is happening, why it matters, and what to do next.

### Component table

| Component | What it is | What it adds | What to look for | What breaks if it's weak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Design intuition** | The starting point. What the team thinks might be happening. | Focus. It points to where to look. | A clear hunch written in simple terms | The work drifts or stays too broad |
| **UX metric** | The measurement of user reaction | Evidence. It shows what actually happened | A metric that matches the question (comprehension, usability, etc.) | The signal can't be read or compared |
| **User need** | What the user is trying to accomplish | Meaning. It explains why behavior matters | A clear outcome the user is trying to reach | Behavior gets misinterpreted |
| **Context** | Where and how the interaction happens | Specificity. It anchors the signal to a moment | A defined screen, step, or flow | The signal feels too general |
| **Business goal** | The outcome the work is meant to influence | Relevance. It connects to impact | A clear goal the team is trying to move | The signal doesn't affect decisions |
| **Direction** | What the team should do next | Action. It turns insight into movement | A visible choice or next step | The signal stays informational |

## How a signal takes shape

A signal is ready when:
- the behavior is clear
- the need explains why it matters
- the context is specific
- the metric matches the question
- the direction is visible

If any of these are missing, the signal needs more work.

Signals don't show up fully formed. They get shaped over time.

1. It usually starts with a **hunch**. Something feels off or could be better. That gets written down and made more specific.
2. From there, the team connects it to **what matters**: what the user is trying to do, what outcome needs to move.
3. Then it gets anchored to a **metric** so it can be read and compared.
4. At that point, the signal is clear enough to **use**. The team can put it next to different directions and see what holds up.

## How signals are strengthened

Even a clear signal can be improved. Six techniques tighten it:

- **Clarify the need** → focus on what the user is trying to do
- **Isolate the context** → keep it tied to a specific moment
- **Choose the right metric** → match the measure to the question
- **Connect to a decision** → make the options visible
- **Compare directions** → look at differences side by side
- **Define the next step** → make it clear what moves forward

## What strong vs broken signals produce

When the pieces come together, signals deliver:
- a clear read on what is happening
- a shared view across the team
- visible tradeoffs between directions
- a decision the team can move forward with

The work moves with more clarity and less back-and-forth.

When the pieces don't come together:
- signals feel incomplete
- feedback doesn't connect to decisions
- teams fall back on opinion
- direction keeps shifting

The work keeps moving, but it's harder to know if it's moving in the right direction.

## Quick use

To build a signal:
- write the hunch in simple terms
- connect it to a user need and business goal
- choose a metric that matches the question
- compare real directions
- decide what moves forward

You don't need perfect clarity. You need enough structure to move.

## Failure modes

These are common ways this skill goes wrong in practice:

- **Signal without Direction.** Five components present but no recommended next move. The signal describes the situation but doesn't guide a decision. Always close the signal with what should happen next.
- **Signal without Context.** A metric and a user need without the surrounding situation. The same 70% completion can mean strong activation OR users powering through despite confusion — context names which.
- **Signal without Business goal.** Tying to user need but not to the business pressure the team is under. The signal feels meaningful inside design but doesn't travel.
- **Component mash-up.** Listing the six parts but not connecting them as one signal. The signal becomes a checklist, not a story — and stories are what shape decisions.
