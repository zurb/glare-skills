# Ground in Signals — Reference

Compressed source: Glare | Design Review, Grounding Techniques v1.3 + the G row of the Rubric and Scoring Model.

## Intent

Anchor decisions in evidence instead of opinion. Move the conversation from "I think" to "we're seeing."

## When to use

- When the user is anchoring a design review in evidence instead of opinion.
- When the conversation keeps looping on preference, taste, or assumptions treated as facts.
- When the team has signals or baselines to bring in but isn't using them.
- When Ground scored 1–2 on the SIGNAL rubric.
- When prepping a review where you have signals to introduce at the right moment.

**Don't use when** outcomes still aren't defined (use `glare:review-identify`) or when there's evidence but no recommendation has formed (use `glare:review-navigate`). For building the underlying signals, see `glare:design-signals`.

## Why it matters

Design signals reduce politics and increase credibility. They create shared language across design and business.

When Ground is weak:
- feedback becomes opinion-driven
- people repeat the same points without progress
- assumptions are treated like facts
- people defend their perspective instead of aligning
- decisions feel harder to support

When Ground is strong, the conversation changes. People react to something real, not just what they think.

## What it considers

- whether real data or user feedback is referenced
- whether a baseline or change is understood
- whether signals influence decisions

## What to listen for

- references to user behavior
- data tied to a change or comparison
- moments where direction shifts based on evidence

## What this unlocks

Signals reduce ambiguity and help the group move forward with confidence.

## What breaks if it's weak

The conversation loops. Opinions repeat without progress.

## Connection to the next step

Signals make it easier to form a clear direction.

## What counts as a signal

Signals don't need to be complex. They can be:

- a moment where users hesitate
- a pattern in behavior
- a result from a quick test
- a comment from user feedback
- something the team has already observed

What matters is that the signal reflects reality.

## Scoring (1–5)

| Score | Level | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Leading | Signals drove the conversation |
| 4 | Advancing | Signals referenced and influenced key decisions |
| 3 | Functional | Some signal grounding |
| 2 | Developing | Signals mentioned but not used for decisions |
| 1 | Reactive | Opinion-driven review |

**Metric hint:** Use **Reframe Adoption** — are signal-based reframes landing with the room?

## When to use Ground techniques

- people repeat the same points without progress
- feedback is based on personal preference
- assumptions are treated like facts
- evidence is mentioned but not used
- the group has no shared reference point
- the conversation keeps circling

By the end of Ground, the team should be able to say: what signal matters, what the signal shows, how it relates to the outcome, whether it changes the direction, what decision it helps support.

## Technique chooser

| If… | Use… |
|---|---|
| Opinions are repeating | Bring in one clear signal |
| People cannot tell whether something improved | Compare to a baseline |
| Feedback sounds strong but unsupported | Ask for evidence behind it |
| A signal is mentioned but ignored | Pause and let it shape the discussion |

## The four techniques

### 1. Bring in one clear signal

When a conversation is based only on opinion, it tends to repeat. A single signal can change that — gives the group something real to focus on.

- **Watch for:** repeated opinions without progress; people defending preferences; the same points coming up again.
- **What it does:** anchors the conversation in reality; reduces ambiguity; helps the group converge.
- **Example:** "In testing, users hesitated at this step before continuing."

### 2. Compare to a baseline

Hard to evaluate something without a reference point. A baseline gives context — what existed before and how things are changing.

- **Watch for:** feedback based on preference instead of progress; difficulty comparing ideas; uncertainty about whether something is better.
- **What it does:** provides context; makes progress visible; supports stronger decisions.
- **Example:** "This version improves on where users got stuck in the earlier flow."

### 3. Ask for evidence behind feedback

Not all feedback is based on the same level of insight. Asking for evidence makes that distinction clear.

- **Watch for:** strong opinions without explanation; conflicting feedback; assumptions being treated as facts.
- **What it does:** improves clarity; strengthens feedback; reduces noise.
- **Examples:** "What makes you say that?" / "Have you seen that happen before?"

### 4. Pause when a signal shifts the conversation

Signals only help if the group has time to process them. In fast-moving conversations, new information can be passed over and not influence direction.

- **Watch for:** signals being mentioned but not discussed; the conversation moving too quickly; people continuing with earlier assumptions.
- **What it does:** helps signals land; improves alignment; supports better decisions.
- **Example:** "Given that, does this still feel like the right direction?"

## Coaching for low Ground scores

**Common failure modes:**
- Debating preferences instead of referencing data
- Having signal data but not introducing it
- Letting stakeholder opinion override evidence

**Behavioral drills:**
- "The signal shows ___. That means ___."
- "Before we decide based on preference, let's look at what the data says."
- "The benchmark for this is ___. We're currently at ___. The gap is ___."

**Success signal:** Someone in the room references the signal data in their own reasoning.

## Bottom line

Bring real signals into the conversation. When the discussion is grounded in what is actually happening, feedback becomes clearer, alignment happens faster, and decisions are easier to make.

## Failure modes

These are common ways this skill goes wrong in practice:

- **Waiting for perfect data.** Holding back signals because they're "not statistically significant yet." Signals don't need to be perfect — they need to be real enough to shift the conversation. Bring in what you have; name the confidence level.
- **Reading data without interpretation.** Showing a chart and letting the room interpret it. The room will reach for the interpretation that supports their pre-existing view. Ground means naming what the signal says before the room argues with it.
- **One signal carrying everything.** A single survey result or one A/B test getting cited as proof on a complex question. Pair signals across types (attitudinal + behavioral + performance) when stakes are high.
- **Bringing signals nobody can verify.** Citing "the research" without naming the study, the audience, the metric, or the date. Unverifiable signals get dismissed faster than no signals.
