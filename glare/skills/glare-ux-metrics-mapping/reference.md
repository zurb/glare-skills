# Mapping Design Impact — Reference (v1.32)

## The opening problem

Design work often struggles to connect clearly to business impact. Product teams may improve things like onboarding, usability, and navigation, but leadership still asks:

- Did it improve the business?
- Did adoption increase?
- Did support issues decrease?
- Did users trust the experience more?
- Did the workflow create better outcomes?

Mapping design impact connects UX metrics to the larger business outcomes surrounding the product. UX metrics act as the *measurement layer* that translates user behavior, workflow friction, confidence, and operational performance into clearer business signals. Instead of design living separately from product or business conversations, teams begin connecting:

- user behavior
- emotional signals
- workflow performance
- operational outcomes
- business results

into one clearer system.

As AI increases product velocity, this becomes even more important. Teams now launch more experiments and AI-assisted features than they can consistently evaluate. Mapping design impact creates clearer visibility across that growing complexity.

---

## Design impact starts with Situation

Without the right context, design impact rarely happens. Many teams jump directly into solutions, interfaces, metrics, AI workflows, and experiments. A team may redesign onboarding because conversion dropped. Another may add AI recommendations because leadership wants faster workflows. Someone else may simplify a dashboard because users called it "confusing."

But underneath those requests is usually a larger situation. Users may not trust the workflow. Onboarding may ask for too much too early. Teams may be solving the wrong problem. Internal assumptions may not match real user behavior.

In Glare, **Situations** help teams slow down long enough to understand what is actually happening before the organization rushes into execution. They help teams understand:

- what triggered the problem
- what users are trying to achieve
- what friction stands in their way
- what progress or success actually looks like

This also helps teams identify which UX metrics actually matter for the workflow instead of measuring disconnected signals that lack context.

## Design impact builds through leverage — the four stages

Design impact rarely comes from one isolated screen or one launch moment. It builds through layers across workflows, systems, and decisions over time. Teams evaluate impact across four connected stages, aligned with the four facets of the Decision Map:

| Stage | Decision Map facet | What teams do here | UX metric role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Define | Understand what triggered the problem, where users struggle, what friction exists, what users are trying to achieve. Identify workflow breakdowns, observe hesitation, validate real user behavior before solutions gain momentum. | UX metrics name what signals are worth measuring inside the workflow. |
| **Intent** | Measure | Shape user behavior intentionally through onboarding, navigation, workflows, AI recommendations, interaction design. The goal is guiding users toward clearer and more confident actions. | UX metrics evaluate whether the design intent is actually working — confidence, comprehension, usability, effort, trust, hesitation. |
| **Outcomes** | Focus | Compare outcomes across the experience. Shift from producing outputs toward identifying which workflows actually create stronger user outcomes. | UX metrics compare outcomes consistently across competing workflows and product directions. |
| **Results** | Lead | Connect workflow improvements to larger business outcomes — adoption, retention, conversion, support reduction, operational efficiency. Design becomes less about isolated screens and more about shaping product direction, organizational alignment, operational performance, and long-term business momentum. | UX metrics connect workflow improvements to larger operational and business movement. |

As teams move from Situation to Results, the number of users involved decreases but the business stakes get higher. The work becomes less about isolated screens and more about shaping product direction, organizational alignment, operational performance, and long-term business momentum.

## Design Signals help teams move from guessing to knowing

Design Signals act as the bridge between UX metrics and business impact. UX metrics create the measurable foundation underneath Design Signals. A Design Signal is a clear pattern that helps teams understand what is happening inside the experience — combining UX metrics, workflow behavior, user reactions, operational patterns, and design intuition into a clearer interpretation layer.

Examples that may all become signals:

- hesitation during onboarding
- repeated recovery behavior
- low confidence in AI workflows
- rising effort during checkout
- discoverability problems

Unlike dashboards that only explain what happened later, Design Signals help teams identify problems while workflows are still evolving. This helps teams move from **"I think"** to **"We know"** with stronger evidence and clearer visibility across the workflow.

## Strong design impact creates pull (not push)

Many teams try to *push* design value into the organization after the work is already complete. A team redesigns onboarding. Another simplifies navigation. Someone improves usability or introduces new AI recommendations. The work is presented during a review with the expectation that the value will become obvious once stakeholders see the screens.

But this is often where momentum slows down. Stakeholder reviews become difficult because:

- business goals remain fuzzy
- success was never clearly measured
- workflow friction was never made visible
- teams debate opinions instead of signals

The conversation shifts away from users and toward scope, assumptions, preferences, technical constraints, and organizational risk. Strong ideas slowly get watered down because the workflow problems were never clearly connected to measurable outcomes.

Stronger teams work differently with Glare. Instead of starting with isolated solutions, they begin with a business moment — onboarding, activation, conversion, workflow efficiency, trust in AI recommendations. Then they use UX metrics to expose hesitation, friction, weak comprehension, rising effort, trust gaps. The metrics make the workflow behavior measurable so teams align around visible evidence. The design decisions are *pulled* from visible workflow problems instead of pushed into the org afterward.

This creates a shared language between product, design, leadership, research, and business stakeholders. The conversation shifts from defending ideas to improving visible workflow outcomes together.

## UX metrics translate user needs into business outcomes

Understanding user needs alone is rarely enough to create organizational impact. Teams need to connect those needs to product outcomes, operational performance, and business goals. Many design teams struggle because user needs and business goals feel disconnected. UX metrics create the translation layer.

Examples:

- comprehension may influence onboarding completion
- trust may influence long-term retention
- workflow effort may influence operational efficiency
- discoverability may influence adoption

Most business systems already measure revenue, growth, retention, conversion, and operational efficiency. Design workflows produce a different class of signals — UX metrics measure trust, comprehension, confidence, hesitation, and emotional response across the experience.

---

## Leading indicators vs lagging indicators

Business metrics are often **lagging indicators**. They appear after workflows are released, onboarding is already struggling, users have already abandoned, support requests have increased, adoption has slowed. By the time retention drops, workflow fatigue spreads, operational friction grows, or support costs rise, the underlying experience problems may already be deeply embedded.

As AI speeds up experimentation, teams release more workflows, onboarding variations, AI recommendations, and interface changes than they can consistently evaluate afterward. Leading indicators help teams identify weak patterns before friction spreads further.

UX metrics often act as **leading indicators**. They surface earlier signals like:

- confusion
- hesitation
- weak confidence
- frustration
- rising effort
- workflow fatigue

This gives teams a chance to improve workflows before larger downstream problems spread further. The earlier teams identify these patterns, the easier they are to improve.

---

## Why mapping matters

One of the hardest challenges for design teams is proving impact clearly. Mapping design impact builds organizational credibility. Many teams struggle because business metrics feel disconnected from UX work, leadership only sees release velocity, product pressure pushes validation aside, and stakeholder opinions reopen decisions repeatedly.

Mapping design impact creates stronger visibility into how design decisions influence operational outcomes. Over time, this helps teams strengthen stakeholder trust, improve leadership alignment, support prioritization discussions, reduce subjective debates, and create stronger product reviews.

The goal is not proving that every design decision directly caused business growth. The goal is creating clearer visibility between user experience, workflow behavior, operational performance, and business outcomes — so teams can make stronger product decisions with greater confidence.

---

## What mapping helps teams do

- connect UX metrics to business outcomes
- identify friction earlier
- improve onboarding and adoption
- evaluate AI-assisted workflows
- reduce operational inefficiency
- strengthen stakeholder alignment
- improve roadmap confidence

Over time, UX metrics help organizations create clearer visibility across how user behavior, emotional signals, workflow performance, and operational outcomes influence larger business results. Instead of relying on assumptions or isolated analytics, teams gain a measurable system for guiding product and design decisions with greater confidence.
