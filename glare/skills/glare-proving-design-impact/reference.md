# Proving Design Impact — Compressed (v1.3)

## The thesis

**Design impact becomes visible when decisions connect to outcomes.**

Most teams have no problem showing the work — screens, flows, prototypes, concepts. What's harder is showing what changed *because* of those decisions. That's where design loses leverage.

A direction is chosen. The work moves forward. Results show up later, but the connection between the design decision and the business outcome is hard to explain.

Rebecca Salerno: *"The hardest part for us right now is connecting design to conversions."*

Glare closes that gap by using design signals to connect the **decision**, the **user response**, and the **outcome the business cares about**. A signal gives the team an early read on whether the decision is creating the change they expected.

**Goal:** show how design decisions shape user behavior *before* the final business metric catches up.

---

## Start with the decision

Design impact starts when a decision is made. A team chooses one direction. That decision carries an expectation: something should improve for the user, and that improvement should support something the business needs.

Mirjam Amoah: *"The biggest challenge is figuring out together what design is actually supposed to achieve and how to measure that in a meaningful way."*

Before proving impact, the team needs to be clear about what the decision was meant to change. A strong decision should make these pieces visible:

- what direction was chosen
- what user need it supports
- what UX metric helps read the change
- what business goal it connects to

**When those pieces are clear early, impact becomes easier to explain later.**

---

## Make the connection visible

Most teams struggle to prove impact because the parts stay disconnected:
- the design decision sits in one place
- the user response sits somewhere else
- the business result shows up later in another report

Glare brings those pieces into one chain. To show design impact, connect:

- the **decision** the team made
- the **signal** that shaped or supported it
- the **outcome** that followed

Pablo Zárate: *"Connecting design outcomes to business metrics in a way that isolates design's contribution from other factors."*

That isolation will rarely be perfect. Product work is shared across teams, systems, timing, and market conditions. **But the connection can still be made visible.** A signal helps show why a decision was made, what users showed, and where that response should matter to the business.

---

## Use signals as early proof

Business metrics usually move late. Revenue, retention, conversion, and cost-to-serve often show up after the work has shipped. By then, it's hard to know which decisions helped create the movement.

**Signals give teams something earlier to work with.**

Karline Segan: *"Revenue is a lagging indicator and design needs to articulate our contribution to leading indicators."*

UX metric signals show whether the experience is moving in the right direction *before* the final business outcome appears:

| Leading UX signal | Likely lagging direction |
|---|---|
| Better comprehension | Fewer drop-offs |
| Stronger confidence | Higher completion |
| Improved usability | Less support burden |
| Stronger desirability | Deeper engagement |

**Signals do not replace business metrics. They help explain how design decisions may influence them.**

---

## Track what design actually changes

Design often changes things that are real but hard to explain in business language. Users understand something faster. They feel more confident. A task takes less effort. A choice becomes clearer.

Oluwatobi Okedairo: *"Qualitative value is harder to quantify, which includes reduced cognitive load, better comprehension, or increased confidence. These are real improvements, but translating them into KPIs executives care about takes effort and storytelling."*

Glare attaches those qualities to decisions and UX metrics. Useful signals often show changes in:

- comprehension
- confidence
- usability
- desirability
- trust
- completion

These give the team a clearer way to explain how the experience is changing and why that change matters.

---

## Connect signals to business outcomes

A signal becomes more valuable when it connects to what the business is trying to move. The connection doesn't need to be complicated. It needs to show a clear chain of logic.

**Worked example A:**
- users understand the onboarding step faster
- comprehension improves
- fewer users hesitate or abandon
- activation has a better chance to increase

**Worked example B:**
- users feel more confident choosing a plan
- confidence improves
- plan comparison becomes easier
- conversion has a better chance to improve

The simple chain:

**decision → signal → user behavior → business outcome**

The chain helps leaders understand how design contributes to results without forcing design to claim full ownership of every business metric.

---

## Tell the impact story

Impact does not become useful just because the data exists. It becomes useful when the team can explain it in a way others can use.

Karline Segan: *"We need to learn to translate and narrate all our invisible work into understandable business value or it will not exist."*

Design teams do a lot of invisible work — clarifying needs, reducing confusion, simplifying flows, removing friction, protecting trust. When that work is only shown as screens, the value can disappear.

**The five-beat narrative:**
- what decision was made
- what user signal supported it
- what changed in the experience
- what outcome it was meant to influence
- what the team should do next

This moves the conversation away from taste and toward impact.

---

## Use it in the room

Design impact matters most when decisions are being made. Bring signals into the conversations where direction, funding, scope, and priority are shaped:

- design reviews
- roadmap discussions
- leadership updates
- release decisions
- planning conversations

A signal gives the room something shared to work from. It helps the team explain:
- why a direction was chosen
- what users showed
- what tradeoffs were made
- how the decision connects to outcomes

Emanuele Milella: *"Even if you have data showing design made a difference, convincing others can be harder than producing the results themselves."*

**Proof needs to be usable in the room, not buried in a report.**

---

## Build proof over time

One signal can guide a decision. Multiple signals create a pattern. As signals build across decisions, design impact becomes easier to explain.

Karline Segan: *"Design creates value across systems and over time, but organizations try to measure it in silos and on an individual basis."*

Each signal becomes part of a larger story:
- what the team believed
- what users showed
- what decision was made
- what outcome changed
- what should happen next

**Over time, this creates a proof system.** The team is no longer defending one isolated decision — it's showing how design decisions compound into better outcomes.

---

## What this changes

When design impact is clear, the work carries more weight:

- design decisions become easier to defend
- signals make tradeoffs visible
- outcomes are easier to explain
- leadership conversations become more grounded
- design gains influence because the value is clearer

**Proving design impact is not about claiming credit for everything.** It's about showing how design decisions shape user behavior, and how that behavior connects to product and business outcomes.

That's how design moves from output to impact.

## Quick-reference impact chain

```
[ DECISION ]
       ↓ informed by
[ DESIGN SIGNAL ]                  ← UX metric + intuition + direction + context + user need + business goal
       ↓ shapes
[ USER BEHAVIOR ]                  ← comprehension, confidence, usability, desirability, trust, completion
       ↓ feeds
[ PRODUCT OUTCOME ]                ← adoption, activation, time on task, engagement
       ↓ moves
[ BUSINESS IMPACT ]                ← revenue, retention, conversion, cost-to-serve, loyalty
```
